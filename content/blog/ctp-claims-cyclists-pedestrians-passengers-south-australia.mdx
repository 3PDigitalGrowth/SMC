If the vehicle cannot be identified, the Nominal Defendant steps in. Helmets, seatbelts and intoxication can reduce your payout, and strict notice and court deadlines apply.

Most people assume CTP compensation is for drivers. It is not. In South Australia, people who were not driving are often the ones with the clearest claim, because nobody can argue about how they drove. They are also the people most likely to fall into a few specific traps: a missed police report, a late notice to the insurer, or an assumption that they cannot claim against someone they know.

## Key Takeaways

- Cyclists, pedestrians and passengers claim against the CTP insurer of the at-fault vehicle, not against the driver personally.
- A passenger in a family member's or friend's car can claim if that driver was at fault. The insurer pays, not the driver.
- For a hit and run or unidentified vehicle, the Nominal Defendant is the defendant. A prompt police report and documented, reasonable inquiries are essential.
- Not wearing a seatbelt or helmet carries a presumed 25% reduction under the Civil Liability Act 1936 (SA). Intoxication, including travelling with a driver you knew was intoxicated, can also reduce a claim.
- Notice to the insurer is generally required within six months, and court proceedings generally within three years. Both have exceptions, so do not rely on them.
- Catastrophic injuries are covered by the Lifetime Support Scheme regardless of fault.

## Who can claim CTP compensation when you were not driving?

![Diagram showing who injured non-drivers claim against under CTP](/blog-images/ctp-claims-cyclists-pedestrians-passengers-south-australia/img-1.png)

Cyclists, pedestrians and passengers can all claim if a motor vehicle driver caused the crash. The claim is made against the CTP insurer of the at-fault vehicle, not against the driver personally. Being a non-driver means there is usually no argument about your own driving, which often puts you in a stronger position.

CTP insurance is compulsory and attached to a vehicle's registration. Its purpose is to compensate people injured through the fault of a driver. South Australia's scheme is fault-based. That means you need to show that a driver's negligence caused your injuries. Where two vehicles share the blame, responsibility can be split between their insurers.

There is one important limit. CTP responds to injuries caused by a motor vehicle. A cyclist who falls off alone, with no vehicle involved, generally has no CTP claim. A cyclist who swerves to avoid a car that drifted into their lane may still have one, even without contact. These cases turn on the facts.

### Can I claim against a friend or family member who was driving?

Yes. If you were a passenger and the driver was at fault, your claim is paid by the CTP insurer on that car's registration. It does not come out of the driver's pocket. The scheme is designed for exactly this situation, and claiming is not an accusation against someone you love.

This is the hesitation we hear about most often from passengers. Consider an illustrative example: a teenager is travelling home to Gawler in a parent's or older sibling's car, and the car leaves the road on a wet night. The teenager is injured, and the driver is also hurt and feels guilty. The teenager's family assumes that claiming means suing the driver. It does not. The claim is against the insurer, and the driver's role is largely to give their account of what happened.

That said, the driver's evidence matters, and so does the family's goodwill. Having an independent adviser can help keep the process practical rather than personal.

## What happens if the driver left the scene or cannot be identified?

If the vehicle that hit you cannot be identified, you can generally claim against the Nominal Defendant. You must report the crash to police promptly and show that you made reasonable inquiries to identify the vehicle. Skipping either step can put the whole claim at risk, so start both immediately.

The Nominal Defendant exists so that a hit and run does not leave an injured person with nothing. It can also respond in some cases involving unregistered or uninsured vehicles. It acts much like an insurer, but it is also entitled to test whether you did what could reasonably be expected to find the driver.

### Why the police report matters

A police report fixes the date, place and circumstances of the crash on an official record. It is also the first thing an insurer or the Nominal Defendant will ask for. Delay invites questions about whether the crash happened as you describe. Report it as soon as you are physically able, or ask a family member to do it for you.

### What counts as reasonable inquiries

Imagine a cyclist hit by a vehicle that does not stop on a rural road outside Gawler. The cyclist cannot read the plate. What would reasonable inquiries look like?

- Asking nearby homes and businesses whether they have CCTV or dashcam footage, before it is overwritten.
- Checking for witnesses, including other road users who stopped.
- Posting an appeal on local community pages and noting any responses.
- Giving police every detail you remember: colour, make, damage, direction of travel.
- Keeping a written record of who you contacted, when, and what they said.

The record is the point. If the vehicle is never found, that record is your evidence that you did your part.

## How do helmets, seatbelts and alcohol reduce a CTP claim?

Under the Civil Liability Act 1936 (SA), a court presumes contributory negligence if you were not wearing a seatbelt or helmet, with a presumed reduction of 25%. Travelling with a driver you knew or should have known was intoxicated can also reduce your damages. These are starting points that can sometimes be challenged.

Contributory negligence does not stop a claim. It reduces it. A 25% reduction means you recover three-quarters of what you would otherwise have received. Because it comes off the final figure, it matters most in serious injury cases.

### Helmets and seatbelts

The Australian Road Rules require bicycle riders to wear an approved helmet, and seatbelt use is mandatory for vehicle occupants. If you were not wearing one, the insurer will raise it. The presumption is not always the end of the discussion. The evidence may show, for example, that your injuries would have been the same either way, or that there were circumstances that affect how the Act applies. Do not assume the full reduction is inevitable, and do not assume it can be avoided. It depends on the facts and the medical evidence.

A helmet issue can also be mixed up with a different question: whether the driver was at fault at all. A cyclist who was hit from behind by a driver who failed to keep a proper lookout may still have a strong case on liability, even if the helmet argument reduces the final amount.

### Intoxication and passengers

The Act also deals with intoxication. A passenger who knew, or should have known, that the driver was intoxicated can face a reduction. A pedestrian or cyclist who was intoxicated can face one too, where the intoxication contributed to the crash. These rules are technical, and the exact presumptions and how they can be rebutted should be checked against the current Act. Passengers are often young, may not have known how much the driver had consumed, and may have had no real alternative way home. Those facts matter, and they need to be recorded early.

## How do injury thresholds and the Lifetime Support Scheme affect what you recover?

South Australia uses the Injury Scale Value (ISV) to assess compensation for non-economic loss, such as pain, suffering and loss of enjoyment of life. Minor injuries may fall below the threshold for that part of a claim. Catastrophic injuries are covered by the Lifetime Support Scheme regardless of fault.

The ISV rates an injury on a scale from 0 to 100. The Civil Liability Act 1936 (SA) sets a minimum threshold, and below it no damages for non-economic loss are awarded. Above it, the ISV drives the amount. Thresholds and the value assigned to each point are set by legislation and can be updated, so check the current figures rather than relying on an older article, including this one.

The ISV is only one part of a claim. Economic loss is assessed separately. That includes lost income, loss of earning capacity, medical and rehabilitation expenses, and care provided by family. An injury might rate low on the ISV and still cost someone months of work.

### Catastrophic injuries and the Lifetime Support Scheme

The Lifetime Support Scheme, run by the Lifetime Support Authority under the Motor Vehicle Accidents (Lifetime Support Scheme) Act 2013, funds reasonable and necessary treatment, care and support for people catastrophically injured in motor accidents. Eligibility does not depend on who was at fault. That matters for a cyclist or pedestrian who may be partly to blame. Support through the scheme is a separate matter from a common law damages claim, and the two interact. Get advice on both at the same time.

## What are the deadlines and first steps after a crash?

![Timeline of CTP claim deadlines from crash to court proceedings](/blog-images/ctp-claims-cyclists-pedestrians-passengers-south-australia/img-2.png)

Report the crash to police promptly, notify the relevant CTP insurer (generally within six months), and keep in mind the three-year limit for starting court proceedings. Exceptions exist for late notice and for children, but they are discretionary and uncertain. Treat the deadlines as fixed and act early.

The six-month notice requirement catches people out because recovery takes time. Someone in hospital or rehab for months may not think about paperwork. A late notice is not always fatal, but you are then asking the insurer or a court for flexibility rather than relying on your rights. The three-year period for court proceedings also runs from the date of the crash in most cases, and different rules can apply to children. Check the Limitation of Actions Act 1936 (SA) and the current CTP rules for your situation.

### A practical checklist

1. **Get medical care.** Tell every treating provider the injury came from a motor accident, so it is recorded.
2. **Report to police.** Note the report number and the officer's name.
3. **Take photos.** Capture the scene, road conditions, vehicle damage, your helmet, your bike, your clothing and your injuries over time.
4. **Collect witness details.** Names and phone numbers, even if they seem unimportant.
5. **Keep medical records.** Reports, scans, referrals, prescriptions and discharge summaries.
6. **Keep every receipt.** Treatment, medication, transport to appointments, repairs and replacement gear.
7. **Track lost income.** Keep payslips and a note of the days you could not work.
8. **Write down what happened.** Do it while it is fresh, and include what the driver said.
9. **Be careful with the insurer.** Be polite and factual, but do not give a recorded statement or sign anything before you understand what it does.

## The insurer's first answer is not the final word

The most useful thing I can tell an injured person who was not driving is that an insurer's first position is a starting point, not a verdict. Insurers apply the rules to the facts they have been given. If the facts are incomplete, the answer will be too.

That view comes from how we approach all insurance disputes, not only CTP. In a different area of our work, an abusive husband burned down the former matrimonial home, leaving our client homeless with her children. Her insurer told her she was not covered because the policy had been cancelled for non-payment. We worked through the cancellation process and found it was defective. She received a payout for the building and also the contents.

That was a property claim, not a CTP claim, but the lesson carries over. A refusal, a reduction for a helmet or seatbelt, or an allegation that you did not make reasonable inquiries should be tested against the Act and the evidence, not accepted because it arrived on letterhead.

The second thing I would say is contrary to what many people expect. Non-drivers are often in the strongest position under the scheme, but they lose ground through small administrative failures: a missing police report, no record of inquiries, a late notice, a recorded statement given too early. Most of those are avoidable if someone tells you about them in the first week rather than the sixth month.
