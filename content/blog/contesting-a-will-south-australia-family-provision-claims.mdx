To contest a will in South Australia, you usually either make a family provision claim under the Inheritance (Family Provision) Act 1972 (SA), arguing the will did not adequately provide for your proper maintenance, education or advancement in life, or challenge its validity. Family provision claims generally must be made within six months of the grant of probate.

Being left out of a will, or left with far less than you expected, is painful. It often lands at the same time as grief, and families can fracture quickly. The legal path is narrower than most people assume, and the clock starts earlier than most people realise.

This guide explains the two different ways a will can be challenged, who can bring a claim in South Australia, how the time limit works, what the court weighs up, how the process unfolds, and what executors should do when a claim is possible. It is written for both sides of the table: the person who feels overlooked and the executor who has just been told a claim may be coming.

## Key Takeaways

- A family provision claim and a challenge to a will's validity are two different things, with different arguments, evidence and outcomes.
- Family provision claims are made under the Inheritance (Family Provision) Act 1972 (SA) and focus on whether the will made adequate provision for proper maintenance, education or advancement in life.
- Spouses, domestic partners and children are the most common claimants. Other categories, such as stepchildren, grandchildren, parents and siblings, must meet extra conditions set by the Act.
- The time limit is strict and runs from the grant of probate or letters of administration. Extensions are possible but never guaranteed.
- Most claims are resolved through negotiation or mediation, not a trial.
- Executors should be cautious about distributing the estate once a claim is possible, and should get advice early.

## What Is the Difference Between Contesting a Will and Making a Family Provision Claim?

![Diagram splitting a will dispute into family provision claim and validity challenge](/blog-images/contesting-a-will-south-australia-family-provision-claims/img-1.png)

People say "contesting a will" to mean two quite different things. A family provision claim accepts the will is valid but asks the court to order more provision for you. A validity challenge says the will should not be given effect at all, for example because the person lacked capacity.

The distinction matters because the evidence, the risks and the likely outcomes are completely different.

### Family provision claims

Under the Inheritance (Family Provision) Act 1972 (SA), the court can order that provision be made from an estate where the will, or the intestacy rules if there is no will, has not made adequate provision for an eligible person's proper maintenance, education or advancement in life. The deceased was free to make the will they wanted. The question is whether, in the circumstances, it left an eligible person without the provision the law expects.

This is the route most people actually need. It does not require you to prove anything was wrong with how the will was made.

### Challenges to validity

A validity challenge attacks the document. The usual grounds are:

- **Lack of testamentary capacity:** the person did not understand what a will is, what they owned, or who might reasonably expect to benefit.
- **Undue influence:** someone overbore the person's free will, so the will reflects the influencer's wishes rather than their own.
- **Improper execution:** the formal signing and witnessing requirements were not met.
- **Fraud or forgery:** the document, or a signature, is not genuine.
- **Lack of knowledge and approval:** the person did not truly know and approve the contents, often raised where suspicious circumstances surround the will.

These claims are fact-heavy and evidence-driven. Medical records, the drafting lawyer's file notes and witness accounts are all central. If a validity challenge succeeds, an earlier valid will may take effect, or the intestacy rules apply. That can leave you with a very different result from the one you hoped for, so it is worth knowing what the alternative document says before you commit.

It is common to run both arguments together, with the family provision claim as a backstop. A short early assessment tells you which is realistic.

## Who Can Contest a Will in South Australia?

The Inheritance (Family Provision) Act 1972 (SA) limits who can make a family provision claim. Spouses, domestic partners and children are the core group. Former spouses or partners, and in some cases stepchildren, grandchildren, parents and siblings, may claim only where the Act's specific conditions are met.

Not everyone who feels hard done by has legal standing. That is the first thing we check.

### Spouses and domestic partners

A surviving spouse or domestic partner is a primary claimant. Domestic partner status depends on the definition in South Australian law, so the nature and length of the relationship will be examined if it is disputed.

### Former spouses and former partners

A former spouse or partner may be able to claim in limited circumstances, usually connected to financial dependence or entitlements that continued after separation. These claims are fact-specific, and any property settlement or maintenance arrangement from the separation will matter.

### Children

Children of the deceased can claim, and there is no cut-off at adulthood. Many people assume an adult child with their own income has no case. That is not correct, although the court will look closely at the child's own circumstances, as we explain below.

### Stepchildren, grandchildren, parents and siblings

The Act also provides for some other relatives, but these categories come with conditions. Depending on the category, you may need to show you were maintained by the deceased, cared for them, or made a contribution to their welfare or estate. The precise conditions are technical, and we check every one against the current legislation for the particular claim before advising. If you fall into one of these groups, do not assume you are in or out until someone has read the Act against your facts.

### An illustrative scenario

Imagine an adult daughter who moved back into her mother's Adelaide home to provide care as her mother's health declined. Over several years she reduced her paid work, managed medications and appointments, and covered some household costs. Her mother's will, made years earlier, left the whole estate to another child who lived interstate and was not involved in the care.

The daughter is an eligible claimant as a child. Her care and the sacrifice to her own earning capacity would be relevant evidence, as would her current financial position, the size of the estate and the mother's reasons for the will. Whether she succeeds, and by how much, depends on all of those factors together. The point is that the will being valid does not end the conversation.

## What Is the Time Limit to Contest a Will in SA?

A family provision claim in South Australia generally must be made within six months from the date of the grant of probate or letters of administration. The court has a discretion to allow an extension, but it is not automatic. Acting early protects your position and avoids a costly argument about delay.

The six months runs from the grant, not from the date of death. That surprises many people. It also means the deadline can arrive before you have even seen the will, because the grant may be made without you being heavily involved.

### When can the court extend time?

The court can allow a claim to proceed out of time, but it will want to know why you were late and what the delay has done to others. The kinds of things it considers include:

- the reason for the delay, and whether it was reasonable
- whether the estate has already been distributed
- whether beneficiaries would be unfairly prejudiced
- the strength of the underlying claim

An extension application adds cost, uncertainty and a hurdle you would not otherwise face. If the estate has been paid out to beneficiaries, recovering funds is harder. Treat the limit as a hard deadline.

### Practical steps in the first weeks

If you think you may have a claim, find out whether probate or letters of administration have been granted and when. Ask the executor for a copy of the will. If you are not sure whether an application has been made, we can help you work out the position. If the person died without a will, the rules for [Probate and Letters of Administration](https://www.stevenmclark.com.au/estate-planning) are different, and the same time limit thinking applies to a grant of administration.

Validity challenges have no single neat statutory countdown, but delay still damages them. Evidence fades, witnesses become hard to find, and assets get distributed. Move promptly either way.

## What Does the Court Consider in a Family Provision Claim?

The court weighs the claimant's financial position, the size and nature of the estate, competing claims from other beneficiaries, the relationship with the deceased, and the deceased's wishes and reasons. No single factor decides the case. The question is what a fair outcome looks like in all the circumstances.

### Your financial position and needs

The court looks at your income, assets, liabilities, health, age and earning capacity. An adult child in secure employment with substantial savings faces a harder path than one with disability, caring duties or limited means. Needs are assessed realistically, including future needs such as housing and medical costs.

### The size and nature of the estate

A modest estate with several people depending on it leaves little room. A larger estate gives more scope for provision without defeating other beneficiaries. The nature of assets matters too. If the main asset is the family home that someone else lives in, practical solutions may be narrower.

### Competing claims and other beneficiaries

The court considers the needs and circumstances of everyone with a legitimate interest. A spouse who depends on the estate may rank ahead of an adult child who is financially independent. Claims are weighed against each other.

### The relationship and contributions

The nature of your relationship with the deceased matters. So do contributions, such as years of unpaid care, financial help or work in a family business. Estrangement is also considered, and the reasons for it. Courts look at who was responsible and whether the estrangement was reasonable.

### The deceased's wishes

The deceased's reasons for the will are relevant, particularly if they were recorded in writing or explained to a lawyer. But the court is not bound to follow them. Wishes carry weight where they are rational and supported by the facts. They carry less where they ignore a clear obligation.

## How Does the Claims Process Work?

![Process flow of a family provision claim from assessment to court proceedings](/blog-images/contesting-a-will-south-australia-family-provision-claims/img-2.png)

A family provision claim usually starts with early advice and a request for documents, moves to negotiation or mediation, and only reaches court if the parties cannot agree. Most claims settle before trial. Costs are in the court's discretion, but estates often bear the reasonable costs of a claim that succeeds or settles sensibly.

### Step 1: Early assessment and documents

We start by working out whether you are an eligible claimant, what the time limit position is, and what the evidence looks like. We then ask the executor for the will, the grant, and information about the estate's assets and liabilities. Without a clear picture of the estate, nobody can sensibly negotiate.

### Step 2: Notifying the executor

We put the executor on notice of the claim, often early and in writing. That protects your position and flags that the estate should not be distributed. A measured, professional letter often opens a more productive conversation than an aggressive one.

### Step 3: Evidence

You will usually need to put your financial circumstances in writing, often by sworn statement. That includes income, assets, expenses, health and your connection with the deceased. Evidence about care you provided, or money you contributed, should be gathered while memories are fresh and records are available.

### Step 4: Negotiation and mediation

Most claims resolve here. A mediator helps the parties explore settlement, often with fewer costs and less family damage than a hearing. Settlement is not weakness. It is often the best way to get certainty, preserve some relationships and keep legal costs from eating into the estate.

### Step 5: Court proceedings

If the claim does not settle, proceedings are filed in the appropriate South Australian court and run through the usual stages: pleadings, evidence, and a hearing. The court decides whether provision should be made and in what amount or form. Trials are expensive and slow, which is why we take settlement prospects seriously from day one.

### How are costs usually dealt with?

Costs are in the court's discretion. In practice, where a claim succeeds or settles, the reasonable costs of the parties are often paid from the estate. That is not guaranteed. A claimant whose case fails may be ordered to pay costs. We explain this risk plainly at the start, because it should shape your decision.

## What Should Executors Do When a Claim Is Possible?

Executors should be cautious about distributing the estate while the time to bring a family provision claim is still running or a claim has been foreshadowed. Take advice before paying out beneficiaries. Distributing too early can expose an executor to personal risk and create unnecessary conflict.

Being an executor already means navigating duties that many people have never faced. Our overview of [Executor Duties and Estate Administration in Australia](https://www.stevenmclark.com.au/estate-planning) is a good starting point if you are new to the role. The prospect of a claim adds a further layer.

### Hold off on distribution

If you know of a person who may have a claim, such as a child who was left out, a partner or a dependant, do not assume the time limit has no relevance to you. Once the estate is distributed, you may have difficulty recovering funds if a late claim succeeds. Waiting until the claim period has passed, or until the position is clear, is usually the safer course. Pay what is clearly required, such as debts and funeral costs, but get advice before anything else.

### Stay neutral and keep records

An executor's role is to administer the estate properly, not to take sides. Keep clear records of assets, decisions and communications. Respond promptly and politely to correspondence from a potential claimant. Antagonising someone usually raises the cost and temperature of the dispute.

### Costs and your own position

Executors are not required to defend every claim at any cost. How much to spend on defending a claim and how much to settle is a judgement call, and the court can review whether costs were reasonably incurred. We help executors strike that balance and avoid sinking the estate into a fight.

### Reducing the risk of claims in future

If you are reading this as a will-maker, the best time to reduce the chance of a later claim is while you are alive. Clear reasons, up-to-date documents and thoughtful structuring all help, including options such as testamentary trusts. Our [estate planning lawyers](https://www.stevenmclark.com.au/estate-planning) can talk you through this, and we cover related topics such as Updating or Revoking a Will in Australia and Testamentary Trusts in Australia in separate guides.

## Why We Don't Offer No-Win-No-Fee in Estate Disputes

We do not offer no-win-no-fee arrangements in estate disputes. We believe that clients are better served by transparent costs advice from the start. A no-win-no-fee uplift can only be justified where there is real risk, and that risk is hard to assess honestly at the outset.

Many firms use no-win-no-fee as a way to attract work. We understand the appeal. When you are grieving and anxious about money, "you only pay if we win" sounds like relief. Our position is different, and we think you deserve to know why.

Ethically, a practitioner can only justify a significant fee uplift where there is an appreciable risk to the client's prospects of success. It is often difficult to assess that risk properly at the start of a matter, and it changes as evidence emerges. A fee structure that depends on that early guess is not built on solid ground.

There is also a conflict problem. Imagine you receive a settlement offer partway through the case. A lawyer paid only on success has a direct financial interest in whether you accept or reject it. If rejecting it risks the firm going unpaid, the advice you receive may be shaped, even unconsciously, by that risk. We will not put ourselves in that position. Your decision to accept or reject an offer should rest on your interests alone.

What we offer instead is a frank assessment up front: whether you have a claim, what it might realistically achieve, what the process involves and how costs are likely to be dealt with. We explain the risks as well as the opportunities, including the possibility that costs may be ordered against an unsuccessful claimant. Steven Clark is actively involved in supervising operations and overseeing standards of work, so the advice you get at the start is the standard of advice you get throughout.

We also encourage people to think about whether litigation is the right path at all. Some of the best outcomes we see come from a calm early conversation, a well-prepared letter and a willing mediator, not a hearing. Our clients are people first, and the aim is a result you can live with, not just a result.
