Consent orders in Australia are written parenting or financial agreements approved by a family law court. Once made, they have the same legal force as orders decided after a hearing. They provide enforceability and greater certainty, but the court must first be satisfied that the proposed terms meet the relevant legal tests.

> **Consent orders at a glance:** Court-approved | Parenting, property or financial matters | Usually decided without a hearing | Legally binding and enforceable

Separation often involves agreement on practical issues before either party understands how to make those arrangements legally effective. A handshake, email exchange or jointly prepared spreadsheet may record an intention, but it does not necessarily provide finality.

This guide explains how family law consent orders work, what the court examines and where poorly drafted terms create avoidable risk. It covers both parenting and property matters, including the different position in Western Australia.

## Key takeaways

The essential point is that agreement between former partners is only the starting point. Consent orders require clear drafting, financial disclosure and terms the court is legally permitted to make. They should resolve the actual risks between the parties rather than simply record broad intentions.

- Consent orders are legally binding court orders made with the parties' agreement.
- Parenting orders must promote the child's best interests, while property orders must be just and equitable.
- Informal agreements, parenting plans and consent orders have different legal effects.
- Financial disclosure should be completed before property terms are finalised.
- Property transfers under qualifying family law orders may receive stamp duty or capital gains tax relief, subject to applicable requirements.
- Independent legal advice can identify drafting, taxation and enforcement problems before documents reach the court.

## What are consent orders in Australia?

![Comparison of informal agreements, parenting plans and consent orders](/blog-images/understanding-consent-orders-australia/img-1.png)

Consent orders are orders a court makes after the parties submit agreed terms for approval. The court does not merely register the agreement. It applies the Family Law Act 1975 and may refuse, query or require changes to terms that do not satisfy the relevant parenting or financial test.

Once approved, consent orders generally operate like orders made after a contested hearing. A person who deliberately fails to comply may face enforcement proceedings. In parenting cases, alleged contraventions can produce serious consequences, although the outcome depends on the circumstances and whether a reasonable excuse exists.

Consent orders can address:

- where a child lives and how much time the child spends with each parent
- parental responsibility and decision-making arrangements
- communication, changeovers, travel and other practical parenting issues
- division or transfer of real estate
- payment or refinancing of mortgages and other liabilities
- division of bank accounts, investments and personal property
- superannuation splitting
- payments between former partners
- steps required to implement an agreed property settlement

The federal family law system is administered by the Federal Circuit and Family Court of Australia. Western Australia has a separate Family Court of Western Australia, so residents there must follow that court's procedures and jurisdictional rules.

### Consent orders compared with informal agreements

An informal agreement can be useful evidence of what the parties intended, but it may leave ownership, liability and enforcement unresolved. For example, agreeing that one person will "keep the house" does not specify refinancing, discharge of liability, transfer timing, sale arrangements if finance fails or responsibility for expenses before settlement.

That lack of detail creates practical risk. A lender is not bound by an agreement between borrowers. Unless refinancing occurs, a person who has moved out may remain liable for the mortgage even if the former partner promised to make every repayment.

### Consent orders compared with a parenting plan

A parenting plan is a written, signed and dated agreement dealing with parenting arrangements. It can be flexible and practical, but it is not enforceable as a court order. Under the [Family Law Act 1975](https://www.legislation.gov.au/C2004A00275/latest/text), a court may consider a later parenting plan when dealing with parenting issues.

Consent orders are more appropriate when the parties need enforceable obligations, clearer safeguards or reliable arrangements for schools, travel and changeovers. The right choice depends on the family's circumstances, not simply whether everyone is currently communicating well.

## What can parenting and property consent orders cover?

Parenting consent orders regulate future arrangements for children, while property consent orders finalise financial interests between separated partners. They may be submitted together, but the court applies a different legal test to each category. Drafting should therefore address each issue separately and with enough precision to be workable.

### Consent orders for parenting arrangements

For parenting orders, the child's best interests are the court's paramount consideration. Agreement between parents does not displace that requirement. The court reviews the proposed arrangements using the principles in the Family Law Act, including safety, developmental needs and the child's circumstances.

Effective parenting orders often deal with ordinary routines and predictable pressure points. These can include:

- school terms, weekends and holidays
- birthdays and significant family occasions
- collection locations and transport responsibility
- telephone or video communication
- overseas or interstate travel
- passports and travel documents
- medical, educational and other major decisions
- notice requirements when plans change
- communication methods between parents

Vague wording can undermine an otherwise sensible agreement. An order providing for "reasonable time as agreed" may work while relations are cooperative, but it offers limited certainty when disagreement returns. Clear default arrangements can reduce that risk while preserving flexibility by written agreement.

### Consent orders for property settlement

Property consent orders can [divide assets](/insights/pets-after-separation-australian-family-law), liabilities and superannuation, as well as set deadlines for transfers, refinancing or sale. The court must be satisfied that the overall result is just and equitable. Full and frank financial disclosure remains important even when both parties support the agreement.

A proper property settlement should identify the legal and beneficial interests involved. That may include real estate, businesses, trusts, vehicles, investments, superannuation, debts and other financial resources. It should also account for implementation costs and events that could prevent the preferred arrangement from proceeding.

For example, an agreement that one party retain a property should normally address what happens if that person cannot refinance within the required period. A sale mechanism may need to specify the agent selection process, preparation costs, price decisions and distribution of proceeds.

### Stamp duty and taxation considerations

A property transfer made under qualifying family law orders may be eligible for stamp duty relief under the legislation of the relevant state or territory. Eligibility is not automatic merely because the parties separated. The form of the order, relationship and transferred property can affect the result.

Capital gains tax may also require attention. The Australian Taxation Office explains that a relationship breakdown rollover can defer a capital gain or loss when qualifying assets are transferred under eligible agreements or court orders. Deferral does not necessarily remove the tax consequence permanently. The recipient may inherit relevant cost-base consequences.

Tax and revenue advice should be obtained before orders are signed. Drafting first and checking taxation later can leave the parties with an agreement that is expensive or difficult to implement.

## How to get consent orders

![Steps for applying for consent orders in Australia](/blog-images/understanding-consent-orders-australia/img-2.png)

To obtain consent orders, the parties must negotiate complete terms, exchange relevant information, prepare the court application and proposed orders, sign the documents and file them in the correct court. A judicial officer then reviews the material and may approve it, request clarification or decline to make particular orders.

### Reach a complete agreement

Agreement should cover implementation, not just the preferred final position. In a property matter, that means addressing transfers, payments, refinancing, releases from liabilities, sale defaults and superannuation requirements. In parenting matters, it means creating arrangements that remain workable when routines or communication become difficult.

Mediation, lawyer-assisted negotiation or direct negotiation may help parties resolve disputed points. Consent orders cannot be imposed merely because one party believes a proposal is fair. Both parties must agree to the proposed wording before filing.

### Complete disclosure and checks

Financial disclosure allows each person to understand the property pool and assess the proposed outcome. Relevant material may include title searches, loan statements, bank records, superannuation information, tax documents, business records and valuations.

Disclosure is not a box-ticking exercise. Missing information can distort negotiations and affect whether the orders produce a final, workable result. It may also expose the agreement to later challenge in serious cases involving non-disclosure, fraud or other statutory grounds.

Parenting matters require different checks. Orders should reflect the child's current circumstances and any safety concerns. Existing protection orders, risk allegations, travel restrictions and practical barriers to changeovers must be considered before filing.

### Prepare the application and proposed orders

The parties ordinarily file an Application for Consent Orders and a separate minute containing the precise orders sought. The [Federal Circuit and Family Court of Australia consent orders kit](https://www.fcfcoa.gov.au/fl/forms/app-consent-kit) provides the relevant federal forms and procedural guidance.

Court forms are not a substitute for careful drafting. The application gives the court background information, while the proposed orders state exactly what each person must do. Ambiguity in the minute can later cause disputes over interpretation or enforcement.

Superannuation splitting orders require particular care. Procedural fairness must be provided to the trustee before the orders are filed, and the fund's formal name and relevant provisions must be accurate. Trustee requirements can vary.

### File in the correct court

Most Australian applicants file through the federal court system. Parties in Western Australia should consult the [Family Court of Western Australia guidance on consent orders](https://www.familycourt.wa.gov.au/C/consent_orders.aspx), because separate forms and processes apply.

A filing fee generally applies unless an exemption or reduction is available. Fees can change, so applicants should check the court's current fee schedule rather than relying on an old article or saved form.

### Court review and approval

Consent applications are commonly considered on the documents without the parties attending a hearing. That does not mean approval is automatic. The court can seek further information, require revised wording or reject terms outside its power.

After the orders are made, each party should retain the sealed version and diarise all deadlines. Property transfers, refinancing and superannuation implementation usually require further documents. The court order authorises or requires action, but it does not complete every administrative step by itself.

## What does the court assess before making consent orders?

The court applies statutory tests rather than asking only whether both parties signed. Parenting orders must serve the child's best interests. Property orders must be just and equitable. The court must also have jurisdiction and legal power to make the specific orders requested by the parties.

### Parenting orders and the child's best interests

The court considers the child's safety and welfare within the statutory framework. Proposed orders should be practical for the child's age, schooling, location, health and relationship circumstances. Terms that expose a child or parent to an identified risk may require safeguards or further evidence.

The court can also question orders that are internally inconsistent or impossible to follow. For example, holiday provisions should align with ordinary term-time arrangements and specify which arrangement prevails if dates overlap.

### Property orders and a just and equitable outcome

For property matters, the court considers the parties' property, liabilities, contributions and relevant future circumstances under the Family Law Act. It assesses the proposed outcome as a whole. A mutually accepted division is relevant, but consent alone does not compel approval.

The application must provide enough information for the court to understand the financial position and proposed effect. Failure to identify a major asset, debt or superannuation interest can delay the application and raise questions about disclosure.

### Time limits still matter

Property applications can be subject to statutory time limits following divorce or the end of a de facto relationship. Under the federal Family Law Act, different periods apply to married and de facto applicants. Proceedings outside the applicable period may require permission from the court, and Western Australian rules require separate consideration.

The sensible course is to obtain advice promptly rather than wait for the deadline. Negotiations, valuation questions, refinancing and document preparation can all take time. An unresolved property arrangement can also prolong joint debt exposure and uncertainty about ownership.

## Why legal advice matters when applying for consent orders

Legal advice is valuable because consent orders create lasting rights and obligations, often without a contested hearing testing the evidence. A lawyer can identify missing assets, defective wording, implementation problems and unintended taxation consequences before an agreement becomes a binding court order.

Parties sometimes assume a court-approved agreement must be commercially practical. The court's approval does not guarantee that a bank will refinance a loan, a property can be transferred immediately or a superannuation trustee will accept defective wording. Those issues need investigation before filing.

Legal review should test questions such as:

- Does the court have power to make every proposed order?
- Are all assets, liabilities and superannuation interests identified?
- Can the agreed transfer or payment actually occur?
- What happens if finance is refused or a deadline is missed?
- Are taxation and stamp duty consequences understood?
- Do parenting provisions contain clear defaults?
- Are safety risks and existing orders properly addressed?
- Is there a practical enforcement mechanism?

Independent advice is particularly important where there has been coercive control, family violence, financial dependence, incomplete disclosure or a significant imbalance in bargaining power. Agreement should be informed and voluntary, not the product of pressure to sign quickly.

## The overlooked issue is whether the orders will survive real life

My view is that the quality of consent orders is measured after cooperation stops. Broad terms can appear reasonable at signing yet fail during refinancing, school holidays, an asset sale or an unexpected claim. Good orders anticipate predictable failure points and state what happens next.

This is where factual investigation matters. In one matter, an abusive husband burned down the former matrimonial home, leaving our client and her children homeless. The insurer initially said there was no cover because the policy had been cancelled for non-payment.

We worked through the cancellation process and identified that it was defective. Our client ultimately received an insurance payout for both the building and contents loss.

That matter was not simply about drafting consent orders, and it should not be treated as a promise about another person's outcome. It demonstrates a wider property settlement principle: do not accept the apparent asset position without checking the documents. Insurance rights, liabilities and contingent claims can materially affect what exists to be divided.

The same discipline applies to parenting arrangements. A schedule should be tested against school calendars, travel time, work commitments and likely disagreements. If an order only works while everyone remains agreeable, it has not resolved the underlying risk.
