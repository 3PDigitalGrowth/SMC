An executor is the person named in a will to collect the deceased's assets, pay debts, funeral costs and taxes, and distribute what remains to beneficiaries according to the will. Executors must act honestly, keep records, treat beneficiaries impartially and are personally accountable if they get it wrong, so careful administration and early legal advice matter.

Being named as executor is an honour. It usually arrives at the worst possible time, when you are grieving and the family is looking to you for answers. Most people have never done this before, and the legal duties are rarely explained until something goes wrong.

This guide walks you through the administration journey after probate. It covers what to do first, your core duties, why distribution is often delayed, where personal liability comes from, and when to ask for help. If you still need to obtain the grant itself, that is covered in our separate article on probate and letters of administration. In short, probate is the court's confirmation of your authority to act. This guide starts from there.

## Key Takeaways

- You do not have to accept the role of executor, but you must decide before you start acting as one.
- Your first priority is to protect the estate: find the will, secure assets, arrange the funeral and notify institutions. Do not distribute anything yet.
- Core duties are identifying and valuing assets, paying debts in the correct order, lodging tax returns, keeping records and acting impartially.
- The common law executor's year and state-based family provision deadlines are the main reasons distribution is delayed.
- Executors can be personally liable for their own mistakes, but a careful process and timely advice reduce that risk substantially.
- Disputes, blended families and business assets are the points at which professional legal support matters most.

## What Is an Executor, and Do You Have to Accept the Role?

An executor is the person the will-maker chose to administer their estate. You are not forced to take the role. You can renounce, which means formally giving up the right to act, or in some cases reserve leave so another executor acts first. The key is to decide before you begin acting.

The executor stands in the shoes of the deceased for the purpose of winding up their affairs. The role carries real authority over money and property, and real responsibility to the beneficiaries and creditors. That is why the law holds executors to a high standard, even when they are family members acting unpaid.

### Renouncing the role

If you do not want the responsibility, you can renounce. This is usually done by a formal document filed with the court or provided to the other parties, depending on the state or territory. The critical point is timing. If you have already started dealing with estate assets, such as collecting money, paying creditors or selling property, you may be treated as having accepted the role. Lawyers sometimes call this intermeddling.

People renounce for good reasons. They may live overseas, be unwell, have a conflict of interest, or simply feel unable to manage a family under strain. Renouncing is not a failure. It is a responsible decision, and the estate can still be administered by another named executor or by an administrator appointed by the court.

### Reserving leave

Where a will names more than one executor, one can sometimes step back without giving up the role entirely. The court reserves leave for that person to come in later if needed. This can suit an executor who wants to remain involved in the background, for example if a co-executor is better placed to do the day-to-day work.

If you are unsure whether you have already accepted the role, speak to a lawyer before you do anything else. A short conversation at this point can save a great deal of trouble later.

## The First Steps: Locating the Will, Securing Assets and Notifying Institutions

In the first days, find the original will, secure the deceased's home and valuables, arrange the funeral, and notify key institutions. Do not distribute anything or sell assets yet. Your priority is to protect what exists and gather information, because everything that follows depends on knowing what the estate owns and owes.

### Locate the will and confirm it is the latest

The original will is usually needed to obtain the grant. Check the deceased's home, safe, bank safe deposit box, and with their lawyer or accountant. Many wills are stored with the law firm that prepared them. If you suspect there is a later will, raise it early. A newer will can change who the executor is and who benefits.

If you have not yet reviewed your own arrangements, administering an estate is often the moment families realise the value of up-to-date estate planning and wills. That is a conversation for another day, but it is worth having.

### Arrange the funeral

The executor generally has the right and the duty to arrange the funeral, following any wishes in the will where practical. Funeral costs are normally paid from the estate, and reasonable funeral expenses are generally given priority. Keep every invoice. If you pay from your own pocket before estate funds are available, you can usually be reimbursed, but only if you have the paperwork.

### Secure and insure the assets

Check that the house is locked and that valuables, vehicles and documents are safe. Confirm that building, contents and motor insurance are still in force. Insurance is one of the most overlooked items in the first weeks. Policies can lapse or be cancelled, and some insurers limit cover when a property is unoccupied.

On this point, we have seen how an insurer's first answer can be wrong. In a non-estate matter, a client suffered a serious fire loss and was told by the insurer she was not covered because the policy had been cancelled for non-payment. We worked through the cancellation process and found it was defective. She was ultimately paid for the building and the contents. The lesson for executors is simple. Check the policy history, and do not accept a denial at face value.

### Notify the right institutions

Work through a sensible list and keep a log of who you contacted and when:

- Banks, building societies and credit unions, so accounts can be frozen or managed appropriately
- Superannuation funds and insurers, to ask about death benefits and policies
- Services Australia, for Centrelink, Medicare and related payments
- The Australian Taxation Office (ATO)
- Utilities, telecommunications, councils and landlords
- Share registries, managed fund providers and any business partners

Many institutions require certified copies of the death certificate and your grant. Where documents need to be certified or identity verified, a notary public or authorised witness can help, and we can assist with certified documents and identity verification where required.

Superannuation deserves a special mention. Super does not usually form part of the estate unless the fund pays the benefit to the estate. The fund's trustee often decides who receives the death benefit, subject to the deed and any binding nomination. Do not assume super will be dealt with under the will.

## Core Executor Duties in Estate Administration

Your core duties are to identify and value assets, pay debts and expenses in the right order, lodge tax returns, keep clear records, and act impartially towards every beneficiary. You owe these duties to the beneficiaries and creditors, and the law holds you to a high standard of care.

### Identify and value the assets

Build a full inventory. Include real estate, bank accounts, shares, managed funds, vehicles, personal items, business interests, digital assets, and money owed to the deceased. Look at tax returns and bank statements from recent years. They often reveal assets the family did not know about, such as dividends from a forgotten share parcel.

Valuations matter. Real estate and valuable items may need a professional valuation, both for distribution and for tax purposes. Obtain valuations as at the date of death, and keep the reports on file.

### Pay debts, taxes and expenses in the right order

Before beneficiaries receive anything, the estate must meet its liabilities. These include funeral costs, administration expenses, tax, and debts such as credit cards, loans and utilities. There is an order of priority, and it can matter a great deal if the estate is small or insolvent. Paying one creditor ahead of another can expose you personally. If it looks like the estate may not cover everything, stop and get advice before paying anyone.

### Lodge the tax returns

According to the Australian Taxation Office (ATO), a deceased person's affairs generally involve a final individual income tax return covering income up to the date of death, and separate returns for income the estate earns after death. Executors are responsible for lodging these. The ATO's deceased estates guidance also explains that capital gains tax consequences can arise when estate assets are later sold or transferred, and that special rules apply to a deceased person's main residence, including a time limit within which the home must generally be sold for the exemption to apply in full (see the ATO guidance in the references).

Tax is a technical area where small errors are costly. An accountant working alongside your lawyer is often money well spent.

### Keep records and act impartially

Keep a file. Record every asset, every payment, every phone call and every decision, with the reason. Keep receipts. If a beneficiary later questions what you did, your records are your best protection.

Impartiality is the duty families find hardest. You may be a beneficiary yourself, or the sibling who was closest to the deceased. Your obligation is to administer the will, not to favour yourself or the person you like best. Where interests conflict, say so openly and get advice.

## How Long Does Estate Administration Take? The Executor's Year and Family Provision Deadlines

Estate administration often takes longer than families expect. The common law executor's year gives you roughly twelve months from death to complete the administration, and family provision deadlines can mean you should not distribute until the claim window has closed. Both depend on the state or territory.

### What the executor's year means

The executor's year is a long-standing common law convention. It recognises that collecting assets, paying debts and sorting out tax takes time, so beneficiaries are generally not entitled to demand their share before about twelve months after death. After that point, delay can attract complaint, and in some circumstances beneficiaries may seek interest or ask the court to intervene. It is a guide, not a guarantee. Complex estates run longer, and simple ones can finish sooner.

### Why family provision claims affect timing

Eligible people, such as a spouse, children and sometimes other dependants, can apply to the court for greater provision from an estate if they believe the will did not adequately provide for them. Each state and territory has its own legislation and its own deadline for these claims, and the starting point differs. Some run from the date of death. Others run from the date of the grant.

By way of example, the Succession Act 2006 (NSW) sets a period running from the date of death, while the Succession Act 1981 (Qld) sets a shorter period, also running from the date of death. Victoria's period runs from the grant of probate. Courts can extend time in some circumstances, so the deadline passing does not always end the risk. Because these rules differ and can change, always confirm the current time limit for the relevant jurisdiction before you distribute. We can do this for you quickly.

The practical rule is simple. Do not distribute the estate until the claim period for your jurisdiction has expired, unless you have taken advice and have a specific reason and safeguard for doing otherwise.

### Other causes of delay

- Waiting for valuations or the sale of property
- Missing assets or uncooperative institutions
- Tax lodgements and any ATO queries
- Disputes about the will's validity or meaning
- Businesses that cannot be sold or wound up quickly

Keeping beneficiaries informed makes delay far easier to accept. A short update every few weeks prevents most of the frustration.

## Executor Liability: Where Personal Risk Comes From and How to Protect Yourself

Executors can be personally liable for losses caused by their own errors, such as distributing too early, paying debts in the wrong order, ignoring tax obligations or favouring one beneficiary. Liability is manageable. Careful process, proper notices, good records and timely advice protect you.

### The common sources of executor liability

- **Distributing too early.** Handing out assets before debts are known or before a claim period has ended.
- **Wrong order of payment.** Paying lower-priority creditors or beneficiaries first when the estate is tight.
- **Tax failures.** Missing lodgements or paying out money the ATO is later owed.
- **Conflict of interest.** Buying estate assets, favouring yourself, or mixing estate money with your own.
- **Poor care of assets.** Failing to insure property, or letting a house or investment lose value through neglect.
- **Weak records.** Being unable to show what you did and why.

### An illustrative scenario: distributing too early

Imagine an adult daughter who is named executor of her father's estate. The will leaves the house and savings equally to her and her brother. Wanting to ease the family's grief and avoid conflict, she sells the house and divides the proceeds within a few months of the funeral.

Eight months later, her father's former partner of many years makes a family provision claim, saying she depended on him and was left nothing. The claim is within time. The money has been spent, the brother has used his share to pay off his mortgage, and the daughter is exposed. The court can order provision from the estate, and the executor may find herself defending a decision she made with good intentions.

This is a hypothetical, but it reflects a very real pattern. Nobody did anything dishonest. The problem was timing and a lack of advice. Waiting for the claim period to pass, and checking for potential claimants, would have protected everyone.

### How to protect yourself

- Get advice before the first distribution, not after the last.
- Check the family provision deadline for your jurisdiction and wait for it to pass.
- Use the formal notice and creditor processes available in your state, so that you are protected when you distribute in good faith.
- Where genuinely uncertain about the will or your powers, ask the court for directions.
- Keep estate money in a separate estate account.
- Document decisions, and where possible get beneficiaries' agreement in writing.
- Properly incurred expenses can generally be recovered from the estate, so claim them with receipts.

## Distributing the Estate to Beneficiaries and Finalising Administration

To distribute an estate to beneficiaries, confirm all debts, taxes and expenses are paid, check claim periods have passed, prepare final accounts, then transfer assets and obtain receipts. Only then should you close accounts and finalise tax. Distribution is the last step, not the first.

### Before you distribute

Run through a final checklist:

1. All known debts and funeral costs are paid.
2. Final and estate tax returns are lodged and any tax paid.
3. The family provision claim period has passed, with no claim notified.
4. Administration expenses, including legal and accounting costs, are accounted for.
5. You have kept a reserve for any final costs or tax.

### Gifts, residue and non-cash assets

Specific gifts, such as a particular property or heirloom, are usually dealt with first. Money gifts follow. What remains is the residue, which goes to the residuary beneficiaries. Not every asset is easily split. Where property is left in shares, or a beneficiary wants to keep an asset, you may need to transfer it in kind or sell and divide the proceeds. Be clear about which the will allows.

If a beneficiary is a minor, or a will creates a testamentary trust, different rules apply, and the assets may be held on trust rather than paid out. These arrangements are common in wills prepared with long-term protection in mind.

### Receipts, accounts and closing the file

Prepare a statement showing what came in, what went out and what each beneficiary receives. Ask each beneficiary to sign a receipt and, where appropriate, a release. Keep these with your estate records. Once everything is distributed, close the estate bank account, lodge any final tax returns and retain your file. Records are usually kept for some years in case a question arises.

## When to Get Legal Help: Disputes, Blended Families and Business Assets

Get legal advice when the will is unclear, a family provision claim is threatened, the family is blended, the estate holds a business, or you are unsure of any step. Early advice is usually cheaper than fixing a mistake later, and reasonable legal costs are generally payable from the estate.

### Will disputes and family provision claims

If someone challenges the will or threatens a claim, do not try to manage it alone. Executors have a duty to defend the will in many circumstances, but they must be careful about how estate money is used. A lawyer can help you respond, explore settlement and protect you from costs orders.

On costs, we do not offer no win no fee arrangements. In our view, the uplift they carry is hard to justify when risk is difficult to assess at the outset. They can also place a firm's interests in conflict with the client's when a settlement offer must be accepted or rejected. You deserve advice that is not shaped by how the firm gets paid.

### Blended families

Blended families bring particular risk. Second spouses, stepchildren, children from earlier relationships and former partners can all have competing expectations, and sometimes legal claims. Where family law issues overlap with the estate, such as property settlement matters or de facto relationships, specialist family law advice is essential.

### Business assets

If the deceased owned a business, was a partner, or held company shares, the executor must often keep it running, value it and navigate partnership or shareholder agreements. Business and commercial law advice is important here, because decisions made in the first weeks can affect the business's value and your own exposure.

## The Real Risk for Most Executors Is Kindness, Not Dishonesty

In our experience, executors rarely get into trouble because they are dishonest. They get into trouble because they are kind. They want to relieve the family's pain, avoid a fight, or honour what they believe the deceased would have wanted. That impulse is natural, and it is also where the law is least forgiving.

The typical mistakes are small and well-meaning. An early partial payment to a struggling sibling. A promise that a relative can keep a car. A decision to skip a formal step because everyone seems to agree. Each one feels harmless. Each one can later become the basis of a complaint or a claim, because the law asks what the will and the legislation require, not what the family agreed over a cup of tea.

Our position is that an executor's most useful quality is not speed. It is steadiness. We encourage executors to treat the first month as a time for information, not decisions: collect, list, secure and record. Then decide. Saying to a family, "I am following a process to protect all of us," is not cold. It is the most caring thing an executor can do, because it protects relationships from the damage a later dispute would cause.
