If someone dies without a will in South Australia, their estate is distributed under the Administration and Probate Act 1919 (SA). The spouse or domestic partner and children come first, then parents, siblings and wider relatives, with the Crown last. Someone must apply to the Supreme Court for letters of administration before any assets can be dealt with.

If you are reading this after a death, you are probably tired and trying to work out what happens next. The short version is that the law has a default plan for people who leave no will. It is orderly, but it is a formula. It does not know who cared for the deceased, who is estranged, or who runs the family farm.

This article explains how to confirm whether a will exists, who inherits under South Australian intestacy rules, how letters of administration work, and what can be done if the outcome feels unfair. If you do not have a will yourself, the later sections show what is at stake. Our [estate planning](https://www.stevenmclark.com.au/estate-planning) page covers the wider picture.

## Key Takeaways

- Intestacy means dying without a valid will, or with a will that does not deal with the whole estate. The Administration and Probate Act 1919 (SA) then decides who inherits.
- Search properly before assuming there is no will. Ask the deceased's solicitor, bank, accountant and the Public Trustee, and check the home.
- Spouses, domestic partners and children take first. Parents, siblings and wider relatives only inherit if nobody closer qualifies, and the Crown is the last resort.
- Blended families, separated couples and unmarried partners are where intestacy most often produces results families did not expect.
- Nobody can deal with the main assets until the Supreme Court grants letters of administration. An administrator is appointed by the Court, not chosen by the deceased.
- A family provision claim under the Inheritance (Family Provision) Act 1972 (SA) is time limited, so get advice early.

## What Does It Mean to Die Intestate, and How Do You Check Whether a Will Exists?

Dying intestate means dying without a valid will, or with a will that does not cover the whole estate. Before assuming no will exists, ask the deceased's solicitor, bank, accountant and the Public Trustee, check the home and any safe deposit box, and make inquiries through the Law Society of South Australia.

A will can also fail for reasons other than being missing. It may not have been signed and witnessed properly. It may name beneficiaries who died first. It may deal with only part of the estate. In those cases there is a partial intestacy, and the Act fills the gap. So even a family that finds a will should read it carefully.

### Where to look for a will

In our experience, wills are very often found in the first few places people look, but families stop too early. Work through this list before concluding there is nothing:

- **Solicitors.** Contact any firm the deceased used for property, business or family matters, not only for wills. Many firms hold original wills in safe custody.
- **The Public Trustee.** The Public Trustee in South Australia prepares and stores wills, so it is worth a direct inquiry.
- **Banks and financial institutions.** Some people lodge wills in a bank safe deposit box. Check statements for box fees.
- **Accountants and financial planners.** They sometimes hold a copy or know where the original is.
- **The home and workplace.** Look in filing cabinets, home safes, desk drawers and among insurance and super papers.
- **The Law Society of South Australia.** It can often help circulate an inquiry to member firms. Check its current process.

If you find only a copy, the original may have been lost or destroyed. That raises separate legal questions about whether the copy can be proved. Do not throw it away, and do not assume it is worthless. Get advice before you decide.

If the search genuinely turns up nothing, keep a record of what you checked and when. The Supreme Court Probate Registry may ask what inquiries were made before it will grant letters of administration.

## Who Inherits if There Is No Will in South Australia?

Under Part 3A of the Administration and Probate Act 1919 (SA), the estate goes to the surviving spouse or domestic partner and children first. If there are none, it passes to parents, then siblings, then more distant relatives. If no relative qualifies, the estate passes to the Crown.

![Flowchart of intestacy order from spouse and children to parents, siblings, relatives and the Crown](/blog-images/dying-without-a-will-south-australia-intestacy-rules/img-1.png)

The Act sets a fixed order of priority. A closer class of relatives generally excludes the more distant ones. The exact shares depend on who survives, so treat what follows as the framework rather than a calculation for your own family.

### Spouse or domestic partner

Where a spouse or domestic partner survives and there are no children, the partner is generally entitled to the whole estate. Where there are also children, the partner takes the deceased's personal chattels (household items, personal effects and vehicles, broadly speaking) and a statutory legacy first. The remainder is then divided between the partner and the children under the Act.

The statutory legacy is a fixed dollar amount set under the legislation. It is not the same as a percentage of the estate. For a modest estate it may swallow most of the value. For a larger estate it is only the opening step. We confirm the current figure against the Act for the relevant date of death rather than relying on old figures, because older articles and forum posts often quote outdated amounts.

A domestic partner is defined in South Australian law and generally depends on a genuine couple relationship of a minimum length, or a child of the relationship, or a registered relationship. Whether a particular relationship meets the definition is a question of fact. It is the point most often argued.

### Children

Where there is no surviving spouse or domestic partner, the children share the estate equally. If a child has died before the deceased but left children of their own, those grandchildren generally take their parent's share. Adult children, children from earlier relationships and children the deceased rarely saw all count in the same way. The law looks at the relationship, not the closeness.

### Parents, siblings and wider relatives

If there is no partner and no descendants, the estate moves outwards. Parents are next. Where no parent survives, siblings (and their children if a sibling has died) are next, and then more distant relatives in the order the Act sets out. Each step only applies if the one before it is empty.

This is where family tree work becomes real work. Finding and confirming every entitled relative can take time, especially across interstate or overseas branches.

### The Crown

If no relative qualifies, the estate passes to the Crown. It is uncommon, but it happens, and it is a poor outcome for a friend, carer or charity the deceased may have wanted to benefit. Only a will can direct a gift to someone outside the family tree.

### What falls outside the intestacy rules

Intestacy only governs assets that form part of the estate. Some assets sit outside it:

- Property held as joint tenants passes to the surviving owner by survivorship.
- Superannuation death benefits are paid by the fund trustee, guided by any binding nomination and the fund's rules.
- Life insurance may be paid to a named beneficiary.
- Assets held in a family trust or company are governed by the trust deed or constitution, although control of those entities can still end up in the estate.

So an intestate estate is sometimes smaller than the family expects. It is worth establishing early what actually sits in the deceased's own name.

## Where Do the Intestacy Rules Surprise Families Most?

Intestacy surprises families most in blended families, in couples who separated without divorcing, and in unmarried relationships. The Act applies the same formula regardless of what the deceased would have wanted, so a former partner, stepchildren or a long-term partner can end up with outcomes the family did not expect.

### Blended families

The law recognises a person's own children, not their partner's children. Imagine a man who remarries and lives with his second wife and her two children from a previous relationship. He has one adult child from his first marriage. If he dies without a will, his stepchildren have no automatic entitlement, however long he raised them. His wife and his own child share the estate under the formula, and the arrangement between them may not reflect what he intended.

There is also a timing problem. If the wife later dies, whatever she inherited from him passes under her own will or intestacy, and his child from the first marriage may receive nothing at all. Many blended families only see this when it is too late.

### Separated but not divorced

Separation does not end a marriage. Unless there is a divorce, a legally married spouse may still be treated as a spouse for intestacy purposes. Imagine a person separated for years who never finalised a divorce or a property settlement and never made a will. If they die while living with a new partner, the estranged spouse may have a significant claim, while the new partner's position depends on whether the relationship meets the domestic partner definition.

The Act has rules for dealing with competing partners, and the facts matter a great deal. This is an area where we recommend prompt advice, because the arguments are about dates, living arrangements and evidence.

### Illustrative scenario: the separated spouse

This is a hypothetical example, not a real matter.

Anna and Mark separated amicably four years ago. They never divorced and never formalised a property settlement. Mark moved in with Jess two years ago. He had no will. He dies suddenly, owning a home in his sole name and a modest share portfolio.

Anna, as the surviving legal spouse, is the first person the intestacy rules look to. Jess has to show she meets the definition of domestic partner. Mark's two adult children from his marriage to Anna may also have a position, depending on how the Act divides the estate between a spouse and children. Everyone in that room is grieving. None of them is acting badly. The outcome is simply not the one Mark would have chosen, and it could have been avoided with a short will and a divorce.

### Domestic partners and unmarried couples

South Australian law does recognise domestic partners, so an unmarried partner is not automatically excluded. But the partner may need to prove the relationship, and disputes about whether a couple was living together on a genuine domestic basis are common. Someone in a long-term relationship who lives separately, or whose relationship has been on and off, faces more uncertainty than a married spouse.

## How Do Letters of Administration Work in South Australia?

Letters of administration are the Supreme Court's authority for a person to manage an estate when there is no will. The person usually applies to the Supreme Court Probate Registry, advertises the intended application, and files supporting documents. Once granted, they become the administrator and can collect assets, pay debts and distribute the estate.

![Process diagram of the steps to obtain letters of administration in South Australia](/blog-images/dying-without-a-will-south-australia-intestacy-rules/img-2.png)

Without the grant, banks, share registries and the Lands Titles Office will generally not release or transfer significant assets. Funeral costs and urgent bills may be an exception in some circumstances, but the main assets are effectively frozen until the Court acts.

### Who can apply

The Court prefers applicants who are entitled to benefit from the estate. In practice the order follows the intestacy order of inheritance: the spouse or domestic partner, then children, then parents, then siblings, and so on. Often the people at the front of the queue agree who should apply, but they do not have to. If several people are equally entitled, they can apply jointly or one can be nominated. If no one suitable is willing, the Public Trustee can be asked to act, or a creditor or other interested person may apply.

The Court may also require security from the administrator that they will administer the estate properly. The administrator takes on real legal responsibility, so being asked to apply is a significant decision.

### How an administrator differs from an executor

An executor is chosen by the deceased in their will and gets authority from the will, confirmed by a grant of probate. An administrator is chosen by the Court, usually from among the family, and gets authority from the grant of letters of administration. After the grant, the practical duties are largely the same: collect the assets, pay debts, and distribute according to the law. The key difference is that an administrator has to follow the intestacy rules exactly. There is no will to give them flexibility.

For the duties themselves, including accounts, debts, tax and timelines, we cover them in our article on executor duties and estate administration, which applies equally to administrators.

### What the application typically involves

- Confirming and documenting the death, family tree and the search for a will.
- Preparing an affidavit setting out the deceased's details, assets, debts and family.
- Publishing notice of the intended application so interested people can respond.
- Filing the application with the Probate Registry and responding to any requirements raised.
- Receiving the grant, then collecting and distributing the estate.

Delays usually come from gaps in the paperwork, uncertainty about relatives, or disagreement within the family. Getting the family tree right at the start saves time.

## What Can You Do if the Intestacy Outcome Feels Unfair?

If the intestacy outcome leaves someone without adequate provision, an eligible person can apply to the Supreme Court for family provision under the Inheritance (Family Provision) Act 1972 (SA). The claim is time limited, generally to six months from the grant, so early advice matters.

The Act allows the Court to order that more be provided to an eligible person from the estate. Eligibility is defined in the legislation and generally covers spouses, domestic partners, children and certain other dependants. The Court considers the person's needs, their relationship with the deceased, the size of the estate and the competing claims of others.

This applies to intestate estates as well as estates with wills. That surprises many people. The intestacy formula is not a guarantee of fairness. A stepchild who was supported by the deceased, or an adult child with a disability, may have a stronger case than the formula suggests.

### Time limits

The general time limit is six months from the date of the grant of probate or letters of administration. The Court has a discretion to extend time in some situations, but it is a discretion, not a right. Do not plan around it. Because the clock runs from the grant, it can start before you know about it, so watch for the advertised application.

## How Does a Will Avoid All of This?

A will lets you choose who inherits, who manages your estate and how assets are divided, instead of leaving those choices to the Act. It avoids the need for a court-appointed administrator, reduces room for dispute, and can protect people the intestacy rules overlook, such as stepchildren, friends or a farm successor.

That is the clearest case for making a will. It is not about being morbid. It is about making the decisions yourself while you can, and sparing the people you leave behind from guessing. A will can also name an executor you trust, make gifts to people outside the family tree, set up trusts for young or vulnerable beneficiaries, and deal with a family home or business. It should be updated after major life events such as marriage, separation, divorce, children or the death of a beneficiary. Our article on updating or revoking a will covers that process. A will is also only one piece of planning. Enduring powers of attorney and advance care directives deal with decisions while you are alive but unable to make them, because a will only operates after death. ### Farm and family business owners

Farms and family businesses are where intestacy can do the most damage. The formula divides the estate by family relationship, not by who works the land or runs the business. Imagine a farming family where one child has worked the property for twenty years and two others live in the city. Without a will, the children may each become entitled to a share, and the farm may have to be sold to make the shares work. A spouse's statutory legacy may not stretch far when most of the value is in land.

Company shares, trust control, partnership interests and water or grazing licences all raise further questions. Each has its own rules, and the intestacy formula does not take them into account. A tailored will, combined with the right structures, can name the successor, equalise the outcome for other children in other ways, and keep the business running. ## Why Intestacy Is a Formula, Not a Decision

The point we want you to take from all this is simple. Intestacy is not a punishment and it is not a disaster in every case. For a married couple with grown children and a modest estate, the formula can produce a sensible result. The problem is that you cannot know in advance whether you are in that group.

We hold a firm view here. The real cost of dying without a will is rarely the legal fees. It is the decisions made for you by a formula that cannot see your family. It cannot see the stepchild you raised, the child who cared for you, the partner you never married or the business your family built. Each of those people is invisible to the Act unless the relationship fits its categories.

The second cost is human. The person who has to apply for letters of administration is usually a grieving spouse or child, who then has to deal with the Court, the bank and every other relative at once. A will names someone you chose, who knows they were chosen. Without one, the role can fall to whoever is willing, and that is not always the person best placed to be neutral.

We do not think people need to be lectured about this. Most people who do not have a will have not made a decision. They have simply put it off. The most useful thing we can say is that it is a short appointment, and it is cheaper and easier than almost any alternative.
