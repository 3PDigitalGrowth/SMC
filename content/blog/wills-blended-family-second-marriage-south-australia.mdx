To protect both a new partner and children from an earlier relationship in South Australia, avoid a simple mirror will. Use a will that gives your partner a life interest or testamentary trust over key assets, names an independent trustee, holds the home as tenants in common, and aligns super nominations with the will.

These conversations are rarely only about money. They involve a partner you love, children you have raised, and the fear that one group might lose out to the other. A will that looks sensible on paper can quietly fail the people you care about most.

This guide explains where the standard "everything to my spouse" will goes wrong, what happens to your existing will when your relationship status changes, and which structures balance the competing interests. It is general information for South Australians. If you are planning a wider approach, our [estate planning](https://www.stevenmclark.com.au/estate-planning) page sets out how the pieces fit together.

## Key Takeaways

- A mirror will leaves your children's inheritance in the hands of your surviving partner, who can change their own will at any time.
- Marriage generally revokes an existing will in South Australia, and divorce generally removes gifts to a former spouse. Separation alone does not.
- Life interests, rights of residence and testamentary trusts let you provide for a partner without handing over the capital outright.
- Super and joint property usually sit outside your will, so they can undo your plan if they are not aligned with it.
- Mutual wills and verbal promises are fragile. Clear written structures with an independent trustee are safer.
- A well-documented, fair plan is your best protection against a family provision claim.

## Why the Standard Mirror Will Fails Blended Families

A mirror will leaves everything to the surviving partner, then to each person's own children. In a blended family, that second step depends entirely on the survivor. Once the assets are legally theirs, they can spend them, gift them or leave them to someone else. Your children have no enforceable right to what you intended.

Remarriage is a regular feature of Australian life. The Australian Bureau of Statistics publishes annual data on marriages where one or both partners were previously married in its Marriages and Divorces, Australia release (see References). The point for estate planning is simple: this is a common situation, and the standard will was designed for a first-marriage family.

### An illustrative scenario

This is a hypothetical example, not a real client matter. Consider Margaret and Peter, who marry in their sixties. Margaret has two adult children. Peter has one son. Both sign mirror wills leaving everything to each other, then to their own children. They own the Adelaide home together as joint tenants.

Margaret dies first. The house passes to Peter by survivorship, and her other assets pass to him under her will. Five years later Peter dies. His will leaves everything to his son. Margaret's children, who thought the arrangement would protect them, receive nothing. Peter's son, who was Margaret's stepchild, ends up with the entire combined estate.

Nobody in that story behaved badly. Peter did exactly what his will said. The failure was in the structure, which relied on goodwill and memory rather than legal protection.

The same problem runs the other way. A survivor who remarries can find their own will revoked by that marriage, or may simply change it under the influence of a new relationship. A mirror will promises nothing beyond the first death.

## What Happens to Your Existing Will When You Marry, Separate or Divorce in SA

In South Australia, marriage generally revokes a will made before the marriage, unless the will was made in contemplation of that marriage. Divorce generally removes gifts and appointments in favour of the former spouse. Separation alone changes nothing. Your old will keeps operating until you update it.

### Marriage

Under the Wills Act 1936 (SA), a later marriage will usually revoke an earlier will. There is an exception where the will clearly states it was made in contemplation of the marriage. If you are remarrying and have a will from your first marriage, assume it needs replacing. If the revocation applies and you have no new will, the intestacy rules decide who inherits. Those rules were not designed with your family in mind, and we cover them in our intestacy rules article.

### Separation and divorce

If you separate but do not divorce, a will leaving everything to your estranged spouse remains fully effective. This catches many people out. After a divorce takes effect, the Wills Act generally treats gifts to the former spouse, and their appointment as executor or trustee, as ineffective. The rest of the will usually still stands, which can leave gaps. A property settlement or financial agreement does not automatically rewrite your will either.

### De facto relationships

Starting a de facto relationship does not revoke your will in the way marriage does. That is a trap in the other direction: your will may ignore a partner you have lived with for years. The rules for ending a domestic partnership also differ from divorce, so get advice on your specific facts.

The practical rule is to review your will at every relationship change, not just when you remember.

## Tools That Balance Partner and Children

The goal is to give your partner security without giving them unrestricted control of assets you want your children to eventually receive. Three tools do most of the work: life interests and rights of residence, testamentary trusts, and the way you hold the family home.

### Life interests and rights of residence

A life interest gives your partner the right to benefit from an asset for their lifetime, usually the home or its income. A right of residence is narrower: they can live in the home but do not own it. When the partner dies or moves out, the asset passes to the people you named, typically your children.

This suits couples where the partner needs a secure home but the home is the main asset for the children. The detail matters, because these arrangements cause disputes when they are vague. Your will should answer these questions:

- Who pays rates, insurance and major repairs?
- Can the partner rent the property out?
- What happens if the partner wants to downsize or move into aged care?
- Can the home be sold and the proceeds used to buy another property under the same arrangement?
- What happens if the partner re-partners and the new person moves in?

A life interest that ignores these questions often turns into an argument between a grieving partner and adult children.

### Testamentary trusts

A testamentary trust is created by your will and only starts when you die. The trustee holds assets and distributes income or capital according to your instructions. For a blended family, it can provide your partner with income or the use of assets for life, with the capital reserved for your children.

It can also protect assets if a beneficiary later remarries, separates or faces creditors, because the assets are held by the trustee rather than owned outright. There can be tax advantages, particularly for income distributed to minor children, but the tax treatment depends on your circumstances. Take advice from your accountant before relying on it.

The weakness is the trustee. We return to this below.

### Tenants in common versus joint tenants

How you hold the family home decides whether your will can deal with it at all. Joint tenants own the whole property together, and on one death the survivor takes it automatically. Your will cannot change that. Tenants in common each own a defined share, and each share passes under that owner's own will.

In the Margaret and Peter example, joint tenancy sent the house straight to Peter. Had they held it as tenants in common, Margaret's half could have been placed in a trust for Peter's benefit, then passed to her children. A joint tenancy can usually be severed by registering the change with Land Services SA. Tenants in common is not a complete answer, though. If your will simply leaves your half to your children, your partner could be left with no right to stay. The title and the will must be designed together.

## Assets Outside Your Will: Super, Joint Accounts and Life Insurance

Many of the most valuable assets in a blended family do not pass under a will at all. Superannuation, jointly held accounts and some life insurance follow their own rules. If they point in a different direction from your will, they can undo your plan entirely.

![Diagram of assets passing under a will versus outside it](/blog-images/wills-blended-family-second-marriage-south-australia/img-2.png)

### Superannuation and binding death benefit nominations

Super is not automatically part of your estate. The fund trustee generally decides who receives the death benefit, usually among your dependants or your legal personal representative, unless you have made a valid binding death benefit nomination. A binding nomination directs the trustee to pay as you specify.

Check three things. First, whether the nomination is valid, since there are formal requirements such as witnessing. Second, whether it lapses. Under the superannuation regulations, binding nominations generally lapse after three years unless the fund deed allows a non-lapsing nomination. Third, whether the outcome fits your plan. Paying everything to a new partner may be what you want, or it may defeat your will.

Tax also matters. Adult children are generally not tax dependants, so a super death benefit paid to them can be taxed differently from one paid to a spouse. Speak to your accountant or financial adviser about the numbers.

### Joint accounts and jointly held assets

Joint bank accounts and other jointly owned assets usually pass to the survivor automatically. In a blended family, that can move large sums to a partner with no obligation to pass them on. Review which accounts are joint and whether that is still what you intend.

### Life insurance

Life insurance may be owned personally, held through super, or paid to a named beneficiary. Each route has different consequences, so confirm who the proceeds will actually reach. Also confirm the policy is still in force. In our own practice we acted for a client whose insurer said her policy had cancelled for non-payment. When we examined the cancellation process, it turned out to be defective, and she recovered for both the building and the contents. The lesson for estate planning is that policies are only as good as their paperwork, so check premiums, ownership and nominations rather than assuming.

## Mutual Wills and Informal Promises

Mutual wills are wills made under an agreement that neither person will change them after the first death. They can be enforced in some circumstances, but proving the agreement is difficult and litigation is costly. We are cautious about relying on them and prefer clear written structures.

The idea behind mutual wills is attractive. Two people agree to leave their combined estate to a shared set of beneficiaries and promise not to change it. If the survivor later breaks that agreement, a court may in some circumstances impose a constructive trust over the assets.

The problems are practical. You must prove a binding agreement existed, not just a shared intention. The survivor can still sign a new will, and the remedy only arrives after their death, when your children must sue the estate at their own cost. The arrangement is also vague about what the survivor may do with the assets in the meantime. Can they sell the house, gift money to a grandchild, or pay for aged care?

Informal promises are weaker still. "Dad said I would get the house" is a common refrain in estate disputes, and a verbal promise is rarely enforceable without a fight.

A testamentary trust or life interest does the same job without relying on proof of a conversation. The terms are written into the will, a trustee is bound by them, and your children do not need to prove anything.

## Reducing the Risk of a Family Provision Claim

A blended family will can be challenged by children, stepchildren or a surviving partner who feels inadequately provided for. You cannot prevent a claim, but you can make one less likely to succeed by providing fairly, recording your reasons and getting your plan documented properly.

In South Australia, eligible people can apply to the court for further provision from an estate under the Inheritance (Family Provision) Act 1972 (SA). Eligible applicants include spouses, domestic partners and children, and in some circumstances stepchildren may also qualify. Strict time limits apply, generally six months from the grant of probate, although the court can sometimes extend them. We cover the process in detail in our contesting a will article, so we only summarise here.

Steps that help:

- Provide for your partner and children in a way a court would see as reasonable, not just technically allowed.
- Record your reasons for unequal gifts in a signed statement kept with the will.
- Keep notes of the advice you received and your instructions.
- Tell the people affected what you have done, where practical. Surprise breeds disputes.
- Consider lifetime gifts carefully, since they can be treated as part of the picture.

A claim is not always a sign of greed. Often it is grief with a legal form. A plan that visibly considers everyone makes that harder to justify.

## Other Documents That Must Line Up

Your will is one document in a set. If a partner and you plan to keep your assets separate, a binding financial agreement can record how property is treated if you separate, and it should be drafted alongside the will so the two do not conflict. We cover that in our binding financial agreements article.

An enduring power of attorney and advance care directive matter too. Who makes financial and medical decisions if you lose capacity? In a blended family, that choice can cause real friction between a new partner and adult children. Our article on enduring powers of attorney and advance care directives covers it.

## Why the Trustee Matters More Than the Trust

The best-designed trust still fails if the wrong person runs it. Our view is that in a blended family, the surviving partner should rarely be the sole trustee of a trust that holds the children's inheritance. They have a conflict between their own needs and the children's interests.

Most advice on this topic focuses on the structure: life interest or trust, tenants in common or joint. We think the more important decision is who administers it. A surviving partner acting as sole trustee, with power to apply capital for themselves, can empty a trust the children believed was protected, without breaking any rule.

There are practical alternatives. An independent co-trustee, such as a trusted family member, an accountant or a professional trustee, creates a check without removing the partner's involvement. Limiting the partner's access to income, with capital released only for defined needs, reduces the conflict further.

The second observation is about conversation. Families often fight not over what the will says but because nobody explained it. If your children hear about a life interest from the executor on the day after the funeral, they will assume the worst. Telling them early, even if it is uncomfortable, is part of the protection.
