If your parent has lost capacity and never signed an Enduring Power of Attorney, they can no longer sign one. In South Australia, the family applies to the South Australian Civil and Administrative Tribunal (SACAT) for an administration order for money and property, and a guardianship order for health and lifestyle decisions, where needed.

Most families reach this point the same way. Mum or Dad has dementia, a stroke or a brain injury, and then the bank says no, the aged care provider wants a signature, or a house sale stalls. Nobody did anything wrong. The paperwork simply never got done.

There is still a path through, and it is a well-worn one. It is slower, more public and less in your control than a signed Enduring Power of Attorney (EPA) or Advance Care Directive (ACD). This guide walks through each step in plain language so you know what to expect. If you are weighing up the planning documents themselves, our [estate planning hub](https://www.stevenmclark.com.au/estate-planning) explains them in detail.

## Key Takeaways

- A person who lacks capacity cannot validly sign an EPA or ACD, so the family cannot fix this by getting Mum or Dad to sign now.
- Capacity is decision-specific. A parent may be able to make some decisions and not others, and medical evidence is central.
- For health decisions, a "person responsible" can often consent to treatment without any tribunal order. Accommodation and contested decisions may need a SACAT guardianship order.
- For money and property, banks, buyers and aged care providers will usually want a SACAT administration order before they deal with you.
- SACAT can appoint a family member or the Public Trustee as administrator. Family conflict makes a Public Trustee appointment more likely.
- Putting your own EPA and ACD in place now spares your children this process entirely.

## What does "lost capacity" mean, and who decides?

Lost capacity means a person can no longer understand, retain, weigh and communicate the information needed to make a particular decision. In South Australia, capacity is decision-specific and is presumed unless evidence shows otherwise. Doctors give the medical evidence, but SACAT makes the legal finding when an order is sought.

Capacity is not an on and off switch. Your father might be perfectly able to choose what he has for lunch and still be unable to understand a contract of sale. Lawyers and tribunals look at the decision in front of them, at the time it needs to be made.

That is why a diagnosis alone is not enough. A dementia diagnosis tells us the condition exists. It does not tell us whether your parent can manage a bank account today. Dementia Australia publishes national prevalence data showing how many families are in this position, and the condition affects people very differently from one week to the next.

Who assesses capacity? Usually a GP, geriatrician, psychogeriatrician or neuropsychologist. The best evidence is a recent, specific report that answers the questions SACAT will ask: what decisions can your parent not make, why, and is the impairment likely to continue. A one-line medical certificate rarely helps.

## Why can't Mum or Dad sign an Enduring Power of Attorney now?

A person without capacity cannot validly sign an Enduring Power of Attorney, because signing requires understanding what the document does and who you are trusting. A document signed after capacity has gone risks being invalid and challenged. Joint accounts and informal bank authorities do not fill the gap, and banks often freeze or cancel them.

This is the hardest message to deliver to families. It is tempting to think a signature on the day, with a witness and a kind GP, will solve everything. If capacity to understand the document is genuinely missing, it will not. A defective EPA can be refused by banks and the Land Titles Office, and it invites disputes later.

There are two grey areas worth knowing about:

- **Borderline capacity.** If your parent has fluctuating capacity, a lawyer may be able to assess whether they can understand an EPA on a good day. This needs prompt, careful, documented assessment. It is not a shortcut, and we will tell you plainly if it is not safe.
- **Existing documents.** Check the filing cabinet, the safe, the bank and your parent's GP. Some people signed an EPA or an old Enduring Power of Guardianship years ago and forgot. Older documents may still operate.

### The limits of joint accounts and informal bank authorities

A joint account can look like a solution. If your parent and one child are both named, that child can often keep operating the account. But a joint account only covers the money in that account. It does not give authority over your parent's sole accounts, shares, superannuation or home.

Joint accounts also carry traps. Ownership questions can arise between siblings and in the estate, and banks can restrict an account once they learn of incapacity. Informal arrangements, such as a third party authority to operate or simply using Dad's card and PIN, usually end when the person loses capacity. Banks can also treat that kind of use as a breach of their terms.

## Who makes health and lifestyle decisions without an Advance Care Directive?

Without an Advance Care Directive, health decisions fall to the "person responsible" under the Consent to Medical Treatment and Palliative Care Act 1995 (SA), usually a spouse or partner, then a close adult relative. This covers most medical treatment. Decisions about accommodation or disputed treatment may need a SACAT guardianship order.

![Ladder diagram of the person responsible order for medical consent with a SACAT guardianship branch](/blog-images/parent-lost-capacity-no-power-of-attorney-south-australia/img-1.png)

The "person responsible" scheme is a practical safety net. Hospitals and doctors work with it every day. In broad terms, the hierarchy runs from anyone with legal authority such as a guardian, to a spouse or domestic partner, to an adult relative who has a close and continuing relationship with the patient, and then to others with a close personal connection. A health practitioner decides who fits, usually in discussion with family.

{/* REVIEW: practitioner to confirm the exact order and wording of the person responsible hierarchy and its limits under the Consent to Medical Treatment and Palliative Care Act 1995 (SA) */}

Two points often surprise families. First, the person responsible is expected to decide as the patient would have wished, not as the family prefers. Second, the scheme is about medical treatment. It does not neatly cover where your parent lives, who they see or what services they receive.

### When a SACAT guardianship order is needed

A guardianship order under the Guardianship and Administration Act 1993 (SA) becomes necessary when:

- Family members cannot agree on a major medical decision.
- Your parent needs to move into residential care and is resisting or cannot consent.
- No suitable person responsible is available.
- A provider is unwilling to act on informal family consent.
- Some treatments require tribunal consent in any event.

SACAT can appoint a family member, or the Public Advocate, as guardian and can limit the powers to what is needed. A narrow order is often better than a broad one.

## How do you apply to SACAT for an administration order?

To manage a parent's money and property, you apply to SACAT for an administration order under the Guardianship and Administration Act 1993 (SA). SACAT must be satisfied your parent cannot manage their financial affairs because of a mental incapacity, then appoints a family member, the Public Trustee or another suitable administrator.

### Who can apply

Generally, any person with a genuine interest can apply. That includes an adult child, a spouse, a health or aged care provider, a bank, or the Public Advocate. In practice, family usually lead, and our advice is to apply early rather than wait for a crisis. A person does not need to be the proposed administrator to apply.

### The evidence SACAT needs

Expect to provide:

- A medical report addressing your parent's decision-making ability for financial matters.
- Details of assets, income, debts and accounts, as far as you can identify them.
- Information about the proposed administrator, including their relationship to your parent and any conflicts of interest.
- Family details, so SACAT can notify the right people.
- An explanation of why an order is needed now, such as a pending sale or bond payment.

Good preparation matters. Gaps in the medical evidence and unexplained family conflict are the two most common causes of delay. If something is urgent, you can ask SACAT to consider an interim order.

### What happens at the hearing

SACAT will give notice to your parent and to relevant family members. Your parent is entitled to take part, and the tribunal takes their views seriously, even where capacity is limited. Hearings are less formal than a court, though they are still a legal process with real consequences.

The tribunal asks practical questions. Who is best placed to manage the finances? Is the proposed person trustworthy and organised? Is there family conflict? Is the estate complex enough to call for a professional? It then decides whether to make an order, who to appoint and what conditions to attach, for example record keeping or reporting.

### Family or the Public Trustee?

SACAT often prefers a suitable family member, because family tend to know the person and cost less. The Public Trustee is more likely to be appointed when:

- Siblings are in open conflict.
- No family member is willing or suitable.
- The estate is complex or there are concerns about financial abuse.
- Existing relationships are fractured or the proposed administrator has a conflict.

The Public Trustee charges fees, and it makes decisions according to its own processes. Families lose flexibility and speed, which is a real cost. An administrator also carries legal duties: acting in your parent's interests, keeping money separate and keeping records. Taking the role on is a serious commitment.

## Can you sell the family home and pay for aged care?

You can sell a parent's home or pay an aged care accommodation deposit only if someone has legal authority, which here usually means an administrator appointed by SACAT. Without that order, buyers, lenders, banks and aged care providers will not accept family instructions, however urgent the situation is.

Consider a composite, hypothetical scenario. A daughter finds a good aged care place for her father, who has advanced dementia. The provider needs an accommodation payment to confirm the room. Dad has funds in his sole name and a home worth far more than the deposit, but the bank will not release a cent to her. There is no EPA, and her informal access to his card has been cut off. Her brother lives interstate and thinks Dad should stay home.

The daughter cannot sign a contract of sale, cannot withdraw funds and cannot give the provider what it needs. The practical answer is an urgent SACAT application for an administration order, with an interim order requested if the facts support one. Providers can sometimes agree to short arrangements, but they cannot wait forever.

### Selling the home

If the home is in your parent's sole name, an administrator will need authority to sell, and buyers and the Land Titles Office will typically ask for evidence of that authority. SACAT can add conditions. If the home is co-owned, other owners still have rights, and we need to look at the ownership structure carefully. Any sale must be in your parent's interests, not in the family's convenience.

### Funding aged care

Aged care funding involves means testing and choices about how accommodation is paid. The aged care system has been through major reform, so check current rules with My Aged Care and a qualified adviser before deciding how to structure payment. A legal authority is the starting point, because providers need a person who can sign agreements on your parent's behalf.

### Centrelink nominee arrangements

Services Australia has its own nominee system. A correspondence nominee can receive and discuss your parent's mail, and a payment nominee can manage payments. This is separate from SACAT and is often quicker. It does not give you authority over bank accounts or property, so it is a helpful partial step, not a replacement for an order.

## What happens when siblings disagree?

When siblings disagree, SACAT decides who is appointed, based on your parent's best interests rather than which child is eldest or closest. The Office of the Public Advocate can be involved, and the Public Trustee or Public Advocate may be appointed when family conflict makes a family decision-maker unworkable.

Disagreement is common and understandable. One child sees the parent daily. Another lives away and worries about money. A third suspects the first. Grief, guilt and old history all arrive at once.

Some practical suggestions:

- Share the medical evidence early so everyone is working from the same facts.
- Agree on the problem to solve, such as the bond or the house sale, before arguing about who solves it.
- Consider proposing joint or alternative administrators, or an independent professional.
- Keep a clear paper trail of expenses and decisions.
- Ask your parent's wishes where you can, and record them.

The Office of the Public Advocate (OPA) provides information, can investigate concerns and can act as guardian of last resort. Its role is about your parent's welfare, not taking sides between siblings. If conflict is intense, we usually recommend getting legal advice before filing, because the way an application is framed shapes how the tribunal reads the family.

## What if the parent's will is out of date?

If your parent's will is outdated and they cannot update it, the only route is usually an application to the Supreme Court of South Australia for authority to make or change a will on their behalf. SACAT cannot do this. These applications are uncommon, specialised and need strong evidence.

This is called a statutory will. The court considers what the person would likely have wanted if they had capacity, along with the interests of those affected. It is expensive and rarely the first step. It is worth raising only where the existing will would produce a clearly unfair or unintended result, for example a will that leaves everything to someone who has since died.

If you want to understand what updating or revoking a will normally involves while capacity exists, that is a separate topic we cover elsewhere. The key point here is timing: a will can be updated easily while a person has capacity, and only with difficulty after.

## What this process really costs families

The legal fees are not the biggest cost of going through SACAT. In our view, the real cost is control. Without documents, strangers decide who looks after your parent's money, the process is on a tribunal's timetable, and your family's private circumstances are put in front of a decision-maker.

That is why we take a clear position with families. Do not treat the SACAT application as a last resort. Treat it as a project with a deadline. The families who struggle most are those who wait until the bank, the buyer and the aged care provider all lose patience on the same week. A medical report obtained while your parent can still participate, and a calm conversation among siblings before filing, make the outcome far better.

Another view we hold firmly: joint accounts are not a substitute for an EPA. They can feel like a quiet fix, but they cover one account, can create ownership disputes and can cause real trouble in an estate. If you are using one because it is easier than a proper document, it deserves a second look.

## Putting your own documents in place

The most useful thing most readers can do after reading this is make sure their own affairs are in order. An Enduring Power of Attorney covers finances. An Advance Care Directive covers health and lifestyle wishes and can name a substitute decision-maker. A current will covers the rest. Together, they mean your children never face the process described above.
