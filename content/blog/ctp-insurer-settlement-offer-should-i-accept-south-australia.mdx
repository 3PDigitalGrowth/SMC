Do not accept a CTP settlement offer in South Australia until your injuries have stabilised, a specialist has commented on future treatment and work capacity, and the insurer has explained how it assessed your ISV. Signing a release closes the claim permanently, so an offer is only worth accepting once it reflects your whole, final position.

If you are holding an offer letter and feeling pressure to sign, you are in a common position. Offers often arrive while recovery is still under way, with a deadline attached and a friendly phone call to follow. That does not make the insurer a villain, but it does mean you should slow down.

This guide explains why insurers make early offers, what a South Australian settlement covers, how to spot an offer that is too low, and what to ask before you sign. It also covers counter-offers, what happens if you say no, and how our [Gawler compensation lawyers](https://www.stevenmclark.com.au/gawler-compensation-lawyers) review an offer. It is general information, not advice on your particular claim.

## Key Takeaways

- An early offer is a commercial decision by the insurer, not necessarily bad faith, but it often prices an injury that has not finished developing.
- Settlement is final. Once you sign a release, you cannot go back if your condition worsens or your work capacity drops.
- Check whether the offer deals with future surgery, future treatment, reduced earning capacity and the ISV assessment, each in plain terms.
- Saying no, or making a counter-offer, does not stop the limitation clock. The general period is three years, so keep an eye on the date.
- Get the offer, your medical reports and your pay records reviewed before you sign anything.

## Why do CTP insurers make early settlement offers?

Insurers make early offers because it is cheaper and more certain for them to close a claim before medical costs and lost earnings accumulate. That is a commercial decision, not necessarily bad faith. The problem is timing: an offer made while you are still recovering prices an unfinished injury.

In South Australia, compulsory third party insurance is provided by licensed insurers. At the time of writing these include SGIC, AAMI, Allianz and QBE, though you should confirm the current list for your accident date. Each insurer manages claims against a budget and a set of internal targets. An early, reasonable-sounding offer closes a file, removes uncertainty and caps exposure.

There is nothing sinister in that. Many claims adjusters are fair, professional people doing their job. Some people with a minor, fully resolved injury are well served by a quick, sensible settlement.

The risk is structural. The insurer is the other side of the table. Its job is to resolve the claim at a figure it considers appropriate, not to tell you what else you might be entitled to claim. It will not volunteer that a specialist report could change the picture.

So treat the offer as an opening position. Whether it is generous or stingy depends on information the letter may not contain.

## What does a CTP settlement offer cover in South Australia?

A CTP settlement offer in South Australia generally bundles several heads of damage: non-economic loss (pain, suffering and loss of enjoyment of life), past and future economic loss, and medical and care expenses. Acceptance is final. Once you sign a release, you cannot return for more if your condition worsens.

### Non-economic loss and the ISV threshold

Non-economic loss compensates you for pain, suffering, loss of amenity and reduced enjoyment of life. Under the Civil Liability Act 1936 (SA), injuries are assessed using an Injury Scale Value (ISV), which turns the severity of an injury into a number. In a South Australian motor accident claim, non-economic loss is generally only payable where the assessed ISV is above 10.

That threshold matters. An ISV just under it can mean no payment for non-economic loss. An ISV just over it can mean a meaningful payment. The assessment is not a precise science, and two reasonable assessors can land on different numbers. The rules also depend on your accident date and the scheme in force then, so confirm how they apply to you.

If your offer includes a figure for non-economic loss but no explanation of the ISV behind it, ask for the reasoning in writing.

### Past and future economic loss

Past economic loss is the income you have already lost because of the injury. Future economic loss is the income you are likely to lose, or the earning capacity you have lost, from now on.

Past loss is usually easier to calculate because you have payslips and tax records. Future loss is harder. It depends on your age, your occupation, your qualifications, how your injury limits you and what work you could realistically do instead. Caps and limits can apply to some economic loss claims under the Civil Liability Act, so the headline figure may not be what you expect.

A tradesperson from the Barossa with a shoulder injury and a casual office worker with the same injury have very different futures. An offer that treats them the same should raise questions.

### Medical and care expenses

This head covers treatment you have already paid for and treatment you will need. It can also include care provided by family and friends, equipment, home modifications and travel to appointments, depending on the circumstances. Medicare, Centrelink or a private health fund may have a claim to be repaid from your settlement, so ask how any such amounts will be dealt with.

If your injuries are catastrophic, a different support pathway may apply in South Australia, so check which one fits your situation before assuming a lump sum is the only route.

### Why settlement is final

A release is a legal document. By signing it, you agree that the insurer has no further liability for this accident, including consequences you cannot yet see. If a knee that was "healing well" needs a replacement in four years, the release still stands.

That finality is why timing is everything. It is also why the offer should be tested against the full picture, not against how you feel this month.

## What are the signs a CTP offer is too low?

An offer may be too low if your injuries have not stabilised, if future surgery or reduced work capacity has not been considered, or if the ISV assessment has not been explained. Any one of these is a reason to pause. Together they suggest the offer reflects today's snapshot, not your real future.

Look for these signs:

- **Your recovery is still moving.** If your treating doctors cannot yet say whether you have plateaued, no one can reliably value the claim.
- **Future surgery or treatment is not mentioned.** A recommendation for a procedure, injections or long-term therapy should be reflected in the offer.
- **Work capacity has not been assessed.** If you cannot do your old job at full capacity, or at all, the offer should show how future loss was calculated.
- **The ISV is a number with no reasoning.** You are entitled to understand how the insurer reached its view.
- **There is a short deadline.** Pressure to accept within days, before your reports are in, is a reason to ask for more time.
- **Psychological injury is ignored.** Anxiety after a crash, or after a cycling or pedestrian collision, is a real injury and can affect work and daily life.

Consider a hypothetical Barossa cyclist hit at an intersection. Six weeks after the crash she is offered a lump sum. She is back at light duties and feels optimistic. Months later, a specialist report confirms ongoing restrictions that limit lifting and long periods at a desk. The early offer never contemplated that. Waiting for the report would have changed the conversation, because the claim could then be valued on a fuller picture.

This is not a prediction about your case. It shows why the order of events matters: medical clarity first, valuation second, settlement third.

## What questions should I ask before I sign a CTP settlement?

Before you sign a CTP settlement, confirm that your injuries have stabilised, that every head of loss is accounted for, and that you understand the ISV assessment and the release. If you cannot answer yes to each question below, you are not ready to sign.

![Clipboard checklist of questions to answer before signing a settlement](/blog-images/ctp-insurer-settlement-offer-should-i-accept-south-australia/img-1.png)

Use this checklist:

1. **Have my injuries plateaued?** Has a treating doctor or specialist said my condition is stable, or is further change likely?
2. **Is future treatment covered?** Does the offer reflect surgery, therapy, medication or equipment I may need?
3. **Has my work capacity been assessed?** Is there medical evidence about what I can and cannot do now and in future?
4. **Do I understand the ISV?** Can I see how the insurer assessed it, and does it match my specialist reports?
5. **Are my pay records complete?** Does the offer account for overtime, bonuses, super and reduced hours?
6. **Who is claiming from my settlement?** Are Medicare, Centrelink or my health fund entitled to be repaid?
7. **What exactly does the release cover?** Does it close every aspect of this claim, and am I comfortable with that?
8. **What is the deadline, and is it real?** Can I have more time to gather reports?
9. **Do I need support to decide?** If I lack capacity because of my injury, who is legally able to decide for me?

The last point deserves care. If an injury leaves someone unable to make decisions, the family cannot simply sign on their behalf. An enduring power of attorney made before the loss of capacity can help. Without one, other legal processes, such as an application to the South Australian Civil and Administrative Tribunal, may be needed. Get advice early rather than mid-negotiation.

## Can I negotiate, counter-offer or say no?

Yes. You can negotiate, make a counter-offer or reject an offer outright. None of these ends your claim. The key risk is the limitation period: the general period in South Australia is three years, and the clock keeps running while you negotiate.

### Negotiating and counter-offers

Most offers are a starting point. A counter-offer backed by medical evidence, wage records and a clear explanation of the ISV is far more persuasive than a request for "more". Insurers respond to evidence.

A good counter-offer explains where the insurer has undervalued something, attaches the proof and sets out a reasoned figure. It also keeps a record of what was said, so that later you can show what the insurer knew and when.

### What happens if you say no

If negotiations do not resolve the claim, court proceedings may be necessary. In South Australia, the general limitation period for personal injury is three years under the Limitation of Actions Act 1936 (SA). Some situations extend or alter that period, for example where the injured person was a child. Separate notice requirements can also apply to CTP claims, so confirm early what steps are needed.

The practical point is simple. An insurer's deadline to accept is not the same as a legal deadline to sue. But the legal deadline is real, and missing it can end the claim entirely. Diarise your date from the day of the accident and do not rely on the insurer to remind you.

Saying no does not usually mean going straight to a hearing. Many disputes keep moving through negotiation, with proceedings filed to protect your position. Ask your lawyer how that would work for you.

## Why a first offer is never the last word, and why our fee approach matters

Our view is that an insurer's first position is only a starting point, and that the advice you receive should not be shaped by how your lawyer gets paid. We do not offer no win no fee arrangements. That is a deliberate position, and it bears directly on whether you should accept an offer.

### An insurer's first answer can be wrong

This is not a CTP matter, but it shows the principle. We once acted for a woman whose former husband burned down the family home, leaving her and her children homeless. Her insurer told her she was not covered because the policy had been cancelled for non-payment. That answer sounded final, and many people would have accepted it.

We worked through the cancellation process step by step and found it was defective. She was paid for the building and for the contents. The insurer's first answer was not the right one.

The lesson carries over. A letter from an insurer, whether it says "you are not covered" or "here is our offer", reflects the insurer's view at one moment. It deserves scrutiny, not automatic acceptance.

### Why we do not use no win no fee

Many firms use no win no fee funding to attract work. We do not. Ethically, a practitioner should only use such an arrangement where there is an appreciable risk to the client's prospects of success that justifies the significant uplift in the fee. It is often hard to assess that risk properly at the start of a matter, and harder again as it progresses.

There is a second problem, and it matters most at the moment you are reading this. Under a no win no fee arrangement, a decision about whether to accept or reject an offer can put the firm's interests in conflict with yours. If rejecting an offer risks the firm not being paid, there is pressure, even if unspoken, to advise acceptance. We will not be placed in that position.

When we tell you to accept an offer, or to wait, that advice is about your claim, not our fee. Ask any lawyer you speak to how they are paid and whether that could ever influence their advice on an offer.
