Probate confirms an executor's authority to administer an estate under a valid will. Letters of administration authorise an administrator where there is no will, no valid will or no executor able to act. Both are court grants used to collect estate assets, pay liabilities and distribute the remaining property lawfully.

The distinction sounds technical, but it determines who controls the estate and what evidence the Supreme Court requires. Choosing the wrong application can cause requisitions, extra costs and delays before assets can be collected.

This guide explains the main grant types, when a grant is required, who may apply and what happens after it is issued. Probate procedure differs between Australian states and territories, so local rules must always be checked.

## Key takeaways

Probate and letters of administration serve the same broad purpose: establishing legal authority to administer a deceased estate. The correct grant depends on whether there is a valid will, whether it appoints an executor and whether that executor can act. Obtaining the grant is only one part of the administration.

- Probate is generally sought by an executor named in a valid will.
- Letters of administration usually apply where the deceased left no valid will.
- Letters of administration with the will annexed may be appropriate when a will exists but no executor can act.
- A grant may be unnecessary where assets pass directly to another person or an institution releases a modest balance without one.
- Executors and administrators must identify assets, pay proper liabilities, address tax and distribute the estate correctly.
- Court procedure, terminology and estate law vary between states and territories.

## Summary table

The practical difference between the grants is the source of the applicant's authority. Probate follows the deceased's valid appointment of an executor. Administration follows statutory priority or a court decision. Administration with the will annexed sits between those positions because the will controls distribution, but not the appointment of an available executor.

| Situation | Usual applicant | Typical grant | Distribution basis |

## What are probate and letters of administration?

![Comparison of probate and letters of administration grant types](/blog-images/probate-and-letters-of-administration/img-1.png)

Probate and letters of administration are formal grants made by a state's or territory's Supreme Court. A grant does not transfer every asset automatically. It provides recognised evidence that the named legal personal representative has authority to collect estate property and complete the administration.

### Probate

Probate is the court's formal recognition of a will and the executor appointed by it. The court considers whether the document should be admitted to probate and whether the applicant is entitled to act.

The executor's authority originates in the will, but banks, land registries and other asset holders commonly require the sealed grant before permitting dealings with estate property. The [Supreme Court of Victoria](https://www.supremecourt.vic.gov.au/wills-and-probate/grants-of-probate-and-administration-of-deceased-estates) describes probate as the process for proving the will and confirming the executor's authority.

### Letters of administration

Letters of administration are normally required where a person dies intestate, meaning without a valid will. The court appoints an administrator rather than confirming an executor selected by the deceased.

The applicant does not automatically receive the grant merely because they are related to the deceased. State and territory law establishes an order of priority. The court may require evidence about relationships, beneficiaries, consents, renunciations and the estate's assets and liabilities.

An intestate estate is distributed under legislation, not according to what relatives believe the deceased would have wanted. The result depends on the deceased's family circumstances and the applicable jurisdiction.

### Letters of administration with the will annexed

A valid will can exist without an available executor. The named executor may have died, lost capacity, renounced probate or be unable to act for another reason. The court can then appoint an administrator while preserving the will as the basis for distribution.

This distinction matters. It prevents an estate from being treated as wholly intestate simply because the executor clause has failed.

## When is a court grant required?

![Decision tree for assets that may or may not require probate](/blog-images/probate-and-letters-of-administration/img-2.png)

A grant is usually required when an asset holder needs formal proof of the representative's authority. Solely owned land, substantial bank holdings and investments commonly trigger that requirement. A grant may be unnecessary for jointly owned assets passing by survivorship or assets released under an institution's own deceased-estate policy.

There is no reliable universal asset threshold. Each bank, share registry, insurer and other institution applies its own requirements, which can change. Ask each asset holder what documents it requires before assuming that a court application is necessary.

### Assets that commonly require a grant

A grant is commonly needed to deal with:

- real property owned solely by the deceased
- bank accounts or term deposits where the institution requires a grant
- shares, managed investments and other registered holdings
- debts owed to the deceased
- business interests requiring a recognised legal personal representative
- litigation commenced by or against the estate.

The asset holder's rules and the form of ownership both matter. An asset's value alone does not answer the question.

### Assets that may pass outside the estate

Some property does not fall under the executor's control in the ordinary way. Jointly owned property may pass to the surviving owner, depending on how title is held. Superannuation death benefits are governed by the fund deed, superannuation law and any effective nomination. Life insurance may be paid directly to a nominated beneficiary.

These distinctions can change the estate's real financial position. A person may appear wealthy while holding relatively little in their estate. Conversely, a failed nomination or unusual ownership arrangement may return an asset or benefit to the estate.

### A practical example

Imagine a deceased person owned a home jointly with their spouse, held a sole bank account and had superannuation with a binding nomination. The home may pass to the surviving joint owner, while the super benefit may be paid under the nomination. The bank may still require probate before releasing the sole account.

That is why the asset map should be prepared before selecting the court process.

## Who can apply for the grant?

The executor named in a valid will normally applies for probate. For administration, the eligible applicant is determined by legislation and court rules, commonly by reference to the person's interest in the estate. Competing claims, missing beneficiaries or uncertain relationships can make an otherwise routine application considerably more complex.

### Applying for probate

An executor can accept the appointment and apply, renounce it or, in some circumstances, reserve the right to apply later. Where several executors are named, the application must explain who is applying and the position of the others.

The original will is important. A copy, damaged document, informal document or will containing unexplained marks can require further evidence. The court may ask for affidavits about how the document was signed, stored, altered or found.

### Applying for administration

For an intestate estate, the applicant must establish their entitlement to the grant. A spouse or domestic partner may have priority, but the correct applicant depends on the jurisdiction and family structure. Children, blended families, estranged partners and disputed domestic relationships can change the analysis.

Where a person with prior entitlement is not applying, the court may require that person's consent, renunciation or evidence explaining why they cannot be located or should be passed over.

The [NSW Government executor guide](https://www.nsw.gov.au/family-and-relationships/deaths/after-a-death/will-and-deceased-estate/guide-for-executors/get-a-grant-of-probate-or-letters-of-administration) also distinguishes probate, administration and administration with the will annexed. Applicants should use the procedure for the jurisdiction where the deceased's estate is being administered.

## How does the application process work?

A sound application starts with evidence, not court forms. The representative should locate the latest will, obtain the death certificate, identify family members and prepare an accurate estate inventory. The required notice, affidavits and electronic filing process then depend on the relevant Supreme Court's probate rules.

### Establish the will and family position

Search for the original will, later wills, codicils and evidence of revocation. Contact the deceased's solicitor, inspect secure records and ask close family members about other documents. Do not remove staples, write on the will or attempt repairs.

For intestacy, record the deceased's marriages, domestic relationships, children and other relevant relatives. Names, dates and legal relationships must be supported by reliable documents where required.

### Identify assets and liabilities

Prepare an inventory showing ownership, approximate values and any secured debts. Check property titles, bank accounts, investments, vehicles, business records, refunds, loans and valuable personal property.

Liabilities may include mortgages, credit accounts, funeral expenses, tax, personal loans and administration expenses. Do not assume every demand made against the estate is valid. Each claimed debt should be checked before payment.

### Publish notice and file the application

Some jurisdictions require notice of the intended application before filing. The applicant then files the documents required by the relevant court, which may include the will, death certificate, inventory and supporting affidavits.

In Victoria, probate matters are filed through the Supreme Court's probate system. Other jurisdictions use their own procedures. Court forms should not be copied across state borders.

### Respond to requisitions

A requisition is a request from the probate office for clarification, correction or further evidence. Common triggers include inconsistent names, an unclear family tree, execution concerns, unexplained alterations or an incomplete inventory.

A requisition does not necessarily mean the application will fail. It does mean the court is not yet satisfied. A precise response is usually more effective than repeatedly amending documents without addressing the underlying concern.

### Use the grant to administer the estate

Once issued, the grant can be provided to asset holders. The representative can collect assets, close accounts, sell property where authorised, pay proper liabilities and prepare for distribution.

Detailed estate accounts should be maintained. Estate money should be kept separate from personal funds, and receipts, invoices, valuations and payments should be recorded.

## What affects probate costs and timing?

Probate costs and timing depend on the jurisdiction, estate structure and quality of the evidence. Court filing fees, legal work, valuations, tax advice and property expenses may all arise. Missing wills, disputed relationships, overseas assets and court requisitions generally add work before administration can be completed safely.

It is unwise to promise a fixed completion date before understanding the estate. Even after a grant issues, property sales, tax matters, creditor claims and disputes can delay distribution.

### Costs that may arise

An estate may incur:

- Supreme Court filing and notice fees
- legal fees for the grant and administration
- accountant or tax adviser fees
- property valuation, maintenance and sale expenses
- genealogy or tracing costs
- fees for resealing or dealing with interstate and overseas assets.

Legal fees should reflect the actual work and risk. A straightforward application supported by a clear will is different from a contested application involving capacity allegations or competing administrators.

### Why early distribution can be risky

Beneficiaries often want prompt payment. The representative must first understand the estate's debts, tax position and potential claims. If money is distributed prematurely, the representative may face difficulty recovering it or meeting a later liability.

The [Australian Taxation Office's deceased-estates guidance](https://www.ato.gov.au/individuals-and-families/deceased-estates) explains that tax obligations can continue after death. Depending on the circumstances, the legal personal representative may need to address outstanding returns, estate income and capital gains consequences.

## What duties and disputes should representatives anticipate?

Executors and administrators must act for the estate rather than their personal interests. They should secure property, preserve value, verify liabilities, keep accounts, communicate appropriately and distribute under the will or intestacy law. Conflicts, delay or undocumented decisions can expose the representative to complaints and personal risk.

Common disputes include:

- challenges to the validity of the will
- allegations of incapacity, undue influence or improper execution
- competing applications for administration
- informal or missing wills
- family provision claims
- disputes about ownership of property
- demands to remove or pass over a proposed representative
- disagreement over sales, valuations or distributions.

A caveat may prevent a grant from issuing while a probate dispute is investigated. Caveats should not be used merely to gain negotiating pressure. They have a defined procedural purpose, and an unsupported caveat can create cost consequences.

Contested estates also involve strict jurisdiction-specific time limits. Obtain advice promptly if a grant, caveat or family provision claim is under consideration.

## The grant is an authority document, not an estate plan

Our practical view is that too much attention is placed on obtaining the grant and too little on the decisions surrounding it. The better approach is to map ownership, authority, liabilities, tax and distribution together. A technically correct grant application does not cure a poorly managed estate administration.

We treat the matter as two connected workstreams. The first establishes authority. The second governs what the representative does with that authority.

Before filing, the representative should be able to answer:

- Which assets form part of the estate?
- Which assets pass outside it?
- Who has authority now, and who will have authority after the grant?
- Are there defects in the will or evidence?
- What debts and tax issues require investigation?
- Is there a realistic risk of a claim?
- What must occur before distribution is safe?

This approach often identifies problems that a form-only service can miss. A grant may be uncomplicated while the administration is not.
